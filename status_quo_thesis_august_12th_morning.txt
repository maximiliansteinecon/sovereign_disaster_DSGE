\begin{abstract}
We develope an extension of the New Keynesian DSGE model by \textcite{Isore2017} and include capital-good producers, entrepreneurs facing an external finance premium, a bond holding banking block and an applied bond disaster risk term.  Calibrated to the euro-area, we find that sovereign disaster risk, priced continuously through bank balance sheets rather than only through the household's Euler equation, produces an amplification that is monotonic in home-bias exposure for consumption, investment, and spreads.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sovereign defaults have a close historical association with emerging economies, arising from a notable spiraling of crises related to debt across Latin American states in the 1980's and a combination of a high global level of interest rates and amounts of borrowing resulted in long-reaching defaults \parencite{Ams2018}. Further, the Mexican peso crisis of 1994/95, in which heavy reliance on short-term, dollar-indexed sovereign debt (tesobonos) left the government unable to roll over its obligations as investor confidence collapsed, forcing a sharp devaluation of the peso that was followed by a spike in inflation and a deep recession, with a formal default averted only by an emergency US Treasury–IMF financing package \parencite{Mishkin1999}. However, this does not only apply to emerging economies as the euro-area sovereign debt crisis of 2010–2012 revealed a fundamental vulnerability in the architecture of monetary unions when member states were facing increased borrowing costs and their solvency has been questioned. When perceived default risk on Greek, Italian, or Spanish sovereign bonds rose sharply, banks holding large domestic bond portfolios suffered mark-to-market losses, tightened credit supply, and amplified the contraction in real activity \parencite{Bocola2016, Perego2020}. This paper wants to investigate the implications on the business cycle of such crises, even if they do not realise, but only their probability of occurrence increased and perceived by agents. However, standard New Keynesian (NK) DSGE models, were not designed to capture this effect on their transmission channels. Even frameworks augmented with financial frictions \parencite{Bernanke1999, Gertler2010, Christiano2014} treat sovereign debt as effectively risk-free and model sovereign shocks as normally distributed or at most mildly fat-tailed. The aforementioned periods show that default risk interacts with borrowing and interest rate increases which could have been observed in economies with heterogeneity in size and wealth level. We therefore raise the question, whether routed tail risk through a bank balance sheet change the amplification of disaster-risk shocks compared to the household Euler in a monetary economy? Therefore, this paper develops a framework in which sovereign tail risk is priced by bondholders generally via the SDF and bond pricing equation, absorbed by bank balance sheets, and amplified into macro tail outcomes through financial frictions and policy feedbacks. Therefore, we extend a NK DSGE model by \textcite{Isore2017} with a banking block à la \textcite{Gertler2011} integrating the external finance premium developed by \textcite{Bernanke1999}.

We find that hat sovereign disaster risk, priced continuously through bank balance sheets rather than only through the household’s Euler equation, produces an amplification that is monotonic in home-bias exposure for consumption, investment, and spreads

Finally, this thesis is motivated by the constant development of specialised DSGE satelite models for euro-area monetary policy model development. As the main monetary policy authority within the Eurosystem of Central Banks, the European Central Bank itself uses structural, semi-structural and a wide range of empirical models \textcite{Cicarelli2024}. While the ECB-BASE acts as a semi-structural model for the euro area \parencite{Angelini2019}, the NAWM developed by \textcite{Coenen2008} is the structural DSGE model that in its first version extended \textcite{Christiano2005} and \textcite{Smets2003}, but has been regardede as obsolete after publication, due to non-inclusion of macro-financial frictions. \textcite{Coenen2018}, who's calibration values we make partially use of in Section \ref{secmain:calibrationandresults}, have updated this model by incorporating a financial sector that can account for financial frictions impacts on economic variables. This modern version is supported by specialised DSGE models to complement the forecasting assessments for the institutions macroeconomic projections. However, as no such work of a sovereign default risk model exists, we want to develope a model that in its foundations could be used as such. 

The paper is organized as follows: Section 1 develops the model, Section 2 discusses the calibration. Section 3 presents the obtained results from the model and discusses them further. Section 4 presents the results from conducted robustness checks. Section 5 concludes.

\paragraph*{Literature Review} The paper is related to the macro-financial literature and builds on four related subbranches. Primarily, it is part of a developed theoretical and empirical literature of macroeconomic models that include economic 'rare disasters' and asset pricing.  A central methodological difference in this literature is whether the probability of disaster is modeled as a fixed, constant parameter, or as a stochastic, time-varying process. This divide organises both the theoretical and the empirical strands reviewed below.

Theoretically, it builds on the developments \textcite{Rietz1988, Barro2006, Gabaix2012, Gourio2012, Isore2017} and intermediate contributions through \textcite{Isore2013} and \textcite{Brede2013}. It originated by \textcite{Rietz1988}, proposing that the equity risk premium puzzle of \textcite{Mehra1985} could be resolved if agents assigned a small probability to a catastrophic consumption collapse. Although this insight was correct in principle, Rietz assumed a fixed disaster probability and provided no macroeconomic production structure, which has left the hypothesis empirically ungrounded. \textcite{Barro2006} supplied the empirical foundation by collecting cross-country macroeconomic data from 35 countries over the twentieth century and documented an annual disaster probability of approximately 1.7\%, with a median peak-to-trough consumption contraction of around 29\%. \textcite{Barro2008} extended the panel to 40 countries and could reinforce the disaster probability value through separating stock market crashes from macroeconomic disasters. However, their calibration was sufficient to match observed equity premia and the low risk-free rate, but retained a constant disaster probability and therefore could not generate time-varying risk premia or business-cycle fluctuations driven by shifts in perceived risk. 

A second wave of the literature relaxed exactly this assumption. \textcite{Gabaix2012} solution was an introdcution of a  time-varying disaster probability and severity through linearity-generating processes, yielding exact closed-form asset prices. His framework introduced an asset-specific resilience term $H_{i,t}$, capturing the expected payoff of asset $i$ conditional on a disaster occurring. Crucially, this framework extends to government bonds: if sovereign bonds partially default in a disaster state, their resilience falls below that of a pure risk-free bond, generating a sovereign risk premium even in the absence of any realised default. \textcite{Gabaix2011} further showed that a standard DSGE can be "disasterized" to inherit rich asset-pricing properties without destroying its macro dynamics, though this clean separation breaks down once financial intermediaries are introduced. \textcite{Gourio2012} was the first to embed a \textcite{Gabaix2011}-style time-varying disaster probability into a DSGE production economy, showing that fluctuations in perceived disaster risk alone can drive business cycles through precautionary saving and endogenous risk premia, without the need for a disaster to even occur. Without New Keynesian elements, this framework abstracted from nominal rigidities and monetary policy. An earlier attempt by \textcite{Isore2013} incorporated a small time-varying disaster probability into a New Keynesian model but restricted its effect to capital depreciation only. Using a simple New Keynesian model, \textcite{Brede2013} incorporates a time-variant and small disaster probability potentially affecting the future economy and shows that even a small increase in disaster probability results in an economy with a recessive state, rooted in limited savings opportunities of the agents. The set-up, however, is constrained by two factors. First, assuming disaster as an absorbing state from the Barro-Rietz model makes it unsuitable for studying time-varying disaster probability, as the economy's disaster state is definite once it enters. Second, the disaster probability captures productivity destruction rather than financial disruption in the set-up, and although nominal rigidities are incorporated, financial frictions are not taken into account. \textcite{Isore2017} therefore added a Calvo pricing \& Taylor rule, and asset-specific sovereign bond recovery into a New Keynesian production economy, showing that rising perceived disaster risk alone, without a realised default, can generate recessions, deflation, and spread widening, making them the first to study time-varying disaster risk transmission in a model with monetary policy and price stickiness. While \textcite{Isore2017} model the sovereign bond pricing channel, they abstract from any explicit banking sector, which e.g. \textcite{Rannenberg2016} did, however, absent of any disaster component. \textbf{This paper introduces the bank balance-sheet transmission as a second, distinct amplification mechanism which has not been done to our knowledge in an existing framework before. This gap within the reviewed literature is what this paper aims to close.}

The empirical evidence for this divide points in the same direction. Under the constant-probability assumption, the size and frequency of disasters can be calibrated but not their variation over time; the time-varying view, by contrast, has direct empirical support. \textcite{Tsai2015} survey both strands and show that state-dependent, time-varying disaster risk is the dynamic that explains not only the equity premium but also violations of the expectations hypothesis in bond pricing and the implied volatility skew in option markets. \textcite{Farhi2016} extend the time-varying framework to an open-economy setting, showing that a single time-varying disaster probability jointly explains exchange-rate excess volatility, the forward-premium puzzle, and carry-trade returns. \textcite{Chen2023} construct a news-based, time-varying global disaster risk index and show it is a significant international predictor of stock and other asset returns, operating primarily through the discount-rate channel. Directly testing the time-varying hypothesis against market data, \textcite{Berkman2011} construct a crisis-severity index from 447 international political crises between 1918 and 2006 and show that its fluctuations move both the mean and the volatility of world stock returns, while \textcite{Manela2017} build a text-based disaster-concern index from over a century of front-page news coverage and find the same pattern. Together, this empirical literature supports time-varying disaster risk as the better-supported specification, which is the choice this paper inherits from \textcite{Isore2017}. In the sovereign literature, this logic extends naturally to default risk. There, sovereign debt becomes the disaster-exposed asset when investors price the possibility of severe losses or partial repayment in adverse states. \textcite{Rebelo2022} combine this directly with sovereign debt, showing in a real business-cycle model with time-varying rare disasters that a country's financial development determines whether its government can credibly commit to repaying through a disaster, since deeper domestic financial markets lower the cost of state-contingent risk-sharing and reduce the incentive to default precisely in the states where default would otherwise be most tempting. Their focus, however, is the sovereign's default decision in a real, closed-economy model without nominal rigidities or a banking sector; this paper instead takes the probability of default as an object continuously priced by bondholders and \textbf{asks how that pricing, rather than the default decision itself, propagates through a monetary economy with financial intermediaries}.

Beyond how the probability process itself is modelled, the literature also separates where disaster risk transmits into the economy. The asset-pricing layer captures how disaster risk raises the sovereign spread even before default occurs, because investors demand compensation for tail losses. \textcite{Gourieroux2021} show that financial instruments whose payoffs are exposed to credit events carry substantial risk premiums, and \textcite{Paluszynski2023} model sovereign default with rare disasters and show that bond spreads co-move with output as markets update their expectations, without explicitly isolating pre-default compensation for tail losses. The second, economic layer, where realised or expected sovereign stress worsens bank balance sheets and amplifies recessions through a financial accelerator channel, is the specific object of the sovereign risk transmission subbranch below.

Secondly, it builds on the sovereign risk transmission and the banking-sovereign nexus. When banks hold sovereign bonds, sovereign risk transmission need not operate solely through higher government borrowing costs: \textcite{Perego2020} show it can instead be modelled through portfolio losses and leverage constraints on the holding institutions themselves, which is the specific mechanism this paper embeds. The euro-area empirical literature consistently documents that sovereign and bank risk move together, especially in crisis periods. \textcite{AnayaLongaric2025} recently studied euro disaster risk and its impact on sovereign bonds, showing that a distinctly procyclical behaviour of investment funds is exhibited when financial-market beliefs about the probability of an institutional rare disaster increase. \textcite{Battistini2014} and \textcite{Brutti2015} document that sovereign yields, bank exposures and systemic risk are tightly linked during the euro crisis: the former show that banks' domestic sovereign exposures respond to both systemic and country-specific components of the yield differential, while the latter use data on cross-country bank exposures to sovereign debt and find that cross-border exposures significantly transmit shocks in sovereign premia. \textcite{Gibson2017} add an important feedback perspective, showing that sovereign spreads, sovereign ratings, and bank ratings can reinforce one another during stress episodes.

A key contribution of the more recent literature in this branch is that it moves from describing correlation to isolating causal mechanisms and, in the most recent contributions, quantifying them structurally. \textcite{Bocola2016} provides the most direct empirical predecessor to the mechanism studied in this paper. Using Italian data from 2007 to 2012, he documents and quantifies two channels through which sovereign risk passes through to real economic outcomes: a funding cost channel, in which heightened sovereign risk raises banks' refinancing costs, and a liquidity channel, in which banks facing balance-sheet stress cut back lending to avoid future capital shortfalls. His structural estimation finds that the funding cost channel accounts for roughly two-thirds of the sovereign risk pass-through to lending spreads, with the liquidity channel accounting for the remainder. \textcite{Bocola2016} is foundational as empirical motivation for the sovereign-bank transmission channel modelled in this paper, but cannot serve as the banking block directly for the following reasons. His model is a heterogeneous-bank partial equilibrium framework calibrated specifically to Italian balance-sheet microdata, without a New Keynesian production structure, monetary policy, or nominal rigidities. It does not incorporate a disaster risk process for the sovereign probability; instead, sovereign risk enters through observed Italian spread dynamics treated as an exogenous driving process.  \textcite{Bocola2016} therefore plays the role of empirical motivation and calibration reference for the sovereign-bank channel parameters, rather than a structural building block.

Each step represents a non-trivial theoretical extension without a guiding precedent in the literature, and the resulting model would depart substantially from the \textcite{Bocola2016} framework.

\textcite{SosaPadilla2018} provides a complementary quantitative precedent, embedding defaultable sovereign debt in a small open economy where domestic banks hold government bonds and a default triggers a banking crisis through the same balance-sheet erosion mechanism modelled here, generating credit contractions of a magnitude consistent with observed sovereign default episodes; his model, however, is a real small open economy calibrated to an actual default event rather than a continuously-priced, possibly-never-realised disaster probability embedded in a nominal DSGE with monetary policy, so it corroborates the empirical relevance of the channel rather than supplying its structure. \textcite{Hur2026} extend this quantitative sovereign-banking literature to the policy dimension, jointly modelling the government's bailout decision and the sovereign's default risk and showing that anticipated bailouts of distressed banks feed back into sovereign credit risk; this paper does not model the bailout decision itself and treats the government's response to a realised default as passive, so \textcite{Hur2026} is relevant chiefly as a demonstration that the banking-sovereign link studied here has first-order consequences for optimal policy, a question left for future extensions of this framework. \textcite{Gennaioli2014} provide the cross-country theoretical counterpart, showing that sovereign defaults are more costly for output precisely where domestic banks hold more government debt, and rationalising this in a model where default destroys bank net worth and collateral value; their cross-sectional evidence that bank sovereign exposure, not the default event alone, drives the output cost of default is part of the empirical motivation for the transmission variable modelled in this paper.

Thirdly, it builds on the DSGE literature with banking frictions and a sub-branch with home bias sovereign bond holdings. The baseline euro-area tradition is represented by \textcite{Smets2003}, which supplies the New Keynesian environment into which financial frictions can be inserted. The hypothesis organising this paper is that time-varying sovereign disaster risk, and not only realised sovereign default, depresses output and tightens credit conditions through two distinct channels. An immediate channel operates continuously through perceived risk: a rise in the perceived probability $\theta_t$ of sovereign default raises the sovereign risk premium demanded by bondholders and compresses bond prices \parencite{Gabaix2012, Isore2017}, independent of whether a default ultimately occurs. A second, lagged financial accelerator channel is a structural feature of the same banking block: were a default realised, losses on sovereign holdings would erode bank net worth, raising the external finance premium on loans to entrepreneurs and firms and amplifying the output contraction through the standard \textcite{Bernanke1999}-style feedback loop. Consistent with the perturbation-based solution method used throughout, and with \textcite{Isore2017}'s own approach, no simulated path in this paper realises a default, so this second channel is discussed as a structural property of the model (Appendix~\ref{sec:banking_block}) rather than quantified among the headline results.

The banking-sector friction literature offers several candidate frameworks for embedding financial frictions into a New Keynesian DSGE with Epstein-Zin preferences and a sovereign bond asset class on bank balance sheets. Two structural requirements guide the comparison below. First, for a sovereign bond to enter the constrained object directly, the friction must generate a microfounded leverage constraint on the asset side of the bank balance sheet. Second, the risk driving the friction must be a plausible candidate for replacement by the time-varying sovereign disaster probability $\theta_t$.

\textcite{Christiano2014} is the most natural first candidate: a large-scale, Bayesian-estimated DSGE combining a \textcite{Bernanke1999}-style external finance premium with a bank capital requirement, in which idiosyncratic uncertainty shocks are found to drive the majority of US business-cycle volatility. However, it fails both requirements.  The bank capital friction is a quadratic adjustment cost on the liability side of the balance sheet, around an exogenous target ratio, rather than a microfounded constraint on asset composition. This means that there is no object in the model that a sovereign bond holding could tighten. Its risk shock, moreover, is a shock to the cross-sectional dispersion of entrepreneur productivity, a fundamentally different source of distress from the sovereign disaster probability $\theta_t$; embedding $\theta_t$ into this framework would require rederiving the full contracting problem under Epstein-Zin preferences with a binary tail event, which would be a separate research contribution in its own right. By contrast, the leverage constraint in \textcite{Gertler2011} emerges directly from the banker's incentive-compatibility condition and applies to total asset value -- exactly the object through which the sovereign bond channel operates here.

\textcite{Gertler2010} and \textcite{Gertler2011} share the same banker-diversion friction and leverage constraint, differing only in that the former adds an interbank market through which banks can also borrow from one another. This interbank layer is structurally irrelevant here: the sovereign-bank channel in this paper operates through realised-default losses eroding an individual bank's own net worth, which is a mechanism operating within the bank, not an across-bank mechanism. More concretely, one of the counterfactual experiments related to the home-bias parameter $\phi=0$ would shut down any interbank amplification equally, making the two mechanisms observationally indistinguishable in the impulse responses. The \textcite{Gertler2011} single-tier specification therefore isolates the balance-sheet channel of interest without introducing a second friction whose identification would require calibration targets not available at the euro-area frequency studied here.

\textcite{Gerali2010} construct an estimated euro-area NK-DSGE with monopolistically competitive banks that set retail rates as markups over a wholesale rate, subject to a quadratic penalty for deviating from a capital-to-asset target. The same objection that rules out \textcite{Christiano2014} applies here: the capital-ratio penalty is not a microfounded asset-side constraint, so no object exists through which a sovereign bond holding could tighten it. \textcite{Gerali2010} also differs in \emph{how} shocks propagate -- primarily through adjustment of retail lending markups rather than through the quantity of credit supplied -- whereas the sovereign bond price $Q^b_t$ in this paper affects credit supply through a binding quantity constraint on total asset value, a mechanism \textcite{Gerali2010}'s pricing-frictions specification does not separate out cleanly enough to attach a sovereign bond asset class to.

\textcite{Rannenberg2016} offers the closest structural precedent for combining two financial frictions in one New Keynesian model, integrating the \textcite{Bernanke1999} entrepreneur accelerator with the \textcite{Gertler2011} bank leverage constraint into a single ``double financial accelerator'' DSGE and showing that the interaction between the two frictions materially amplifies shock transmission relative to either block alone.

Having evaluated the available frameworks against these requirements, we integrate the entrepreneur block of \textcite{Bernanke1999} and the banking block of \textcite{Gertler2011}. \textcite{Bernanke1999} is a canonical, minimally parameterised model of the external finance premium: it adds three equations and two parameters, $\chi^e$ and $\sigma^e$, with well-established calibration targets in the literature, has a closed-form steady state, and has been integrated with other NK models at third-order perturbation \parencite{FernandezVillaverde2018}. The \textcite{Gertler2011} bank block gives the leverage constraint a theoretical foundation through the banker's own optimisation problem, so that bank net worth affected by a disaster is grounded in incentive-compatible behaviour rather than assumed as a reduced form. The decisive argument for combining both, following \textcite{Rannenberg2016}, is the separation of bank net worth $N_t^b$ and entrepreneur net worth $N_t^e$: it allows the sovereign-bank channel through $N_t^b$ and the standard financial accelerator through $N_t^e$ to operate simultaneously. The full derivation of both blocks is provided in Appendix \ref{sec:banking_block}.

Lastly, the paper relies throughout on Epstein-Zin-Weil recursive preferences, which separate risk aversion from the elasticity of intertemporal substitution - a distinction that is not a modelling convenience but the mechanism through which disaster risk affects behaviour at all. Under standard time-additive expected utility, risk aversion and intertemporal substitution are governed by the same parameter, so a disaster-risk shock that raises the conditional variance of future consumption cannot be disentangled from a shock to its expected growth rate. \textcite{Epstein1989} develop the axiomatic foundation for this class of recursive, non-expected-utility preferences and show that they nest nests the predictions of both the consumption CAPM and the static CAPM as special cases while allowing risk aversion and substitution to be set independently. \textcite{Weil1990} apply this framework specifically to a macroeconomic, infinite-horizon consumption problem, introducing the generalised isoelastic form -- combining a risk-aversion parameter $\gamma$ with a separate elasticity of intertemporal substitution $1/\tilde\psi$ -- that \textcite{Gourio2012} and \textcite{Isore2017} adopt directly, and that this paper inherits: the household's Epstein-Zin-Weil stochastic discount factor $Q_t$ enters the banking block's own incentive-constraint objects, $\eta_t^b$ and $\nu_t^b$, directly, so that the bank's leverage limit is itself discounted using the same preference parameters that govern the disaster-risk pricing channel." Without this separation, the sign of the model's central mechanism would be undetermined: as \textcite{Isore2017} show, whether a rise in disaster risk raises or lowers current consumption and investment depends on whether the EIS is above or below unity, a distinction that has no content under time-additive utility.

\section{Theoretical Model}
As reasoned before, the theoretical model in this section builds on \textcite{Isore2017} and is extended by a banking sector combining \textcite{Gertler2011} with home-bias sovereign bond holdings and the \textcite{Bernanke1999} external finance premium. It therefore consists of a representative household with capital-goods producers, a continuum of intermediate-goods firms with Calvo Pricing, a representative final-goods firm, a continuum of entrepreneurs (BGG) in a banking block with a continuum of banks (GK) and a public authority. The following subsection defines the models disaster state. 

\subsection{The Disaster State}

% Uncertainty about the future is central to asset pricing, since all agents are typically risk-averse. A \textbf{\emph{state of nature}} denotes a possible realization of the world at a given point in time. In principle, the set of states of nature is continuous and thus uncountably infinite. 

% Accordingly, we first present a discrete version of the analysis, assuming discrete time $t \in \{0,1,2,\dots\}$ and a finite state space $S=\{s_1,\dots,s_n\}$. To make the analysis tractable, however, we will discretize this set, thereby simplifying reality while still providing a workable framework, in which our economy can enter two states of nature ($n = 2$), namely a disaster and "business-as-usual" (BAU) state. In the latter, the economy operates in conditions where no capital, bonds nor consumption or output are destroyed. However, there exists a small time-varying probability to enter a disaster state in which it experiences e.g. a doom loop where one economic condition creats another, resulting in another economic negative condition which then reinforces the first or second, resulting in a negatively spiraling chain reaction.

Each period, the economy faces a small but time-varying probability of a sovereign partial default. Following \textcite{Isore2017} and \textcite{Gourio2012}, disaster destroys both physical capital and productivity by the factor $(1-\Delta^k)$ when $x_{t+1}=1$. This nests \textcite{Isore2017} exactly at $\phi=0$, providing a non-trivial benchmark against which the sovereign-bank transmission channel is identified. The paper's novel mechanism operates through the bond-pricing channel: disaster additionally imposes a haircut $\Delta^b$ on sovereign bonds ($\Delta^b > 0$), transmitting through bank balance sheets rather than through capital destruction alone.

A binary disaster $x_{t+1}\in\{0,1\}$ is drawn in period $t$. In the BAU state $x_{t+1} = 0$, sovereign bonds are repaid in full. In the disaster state $x_{t+1} = 1$, bondholders receive only a fraction $(1-\Delta^b)$ of the face value.The disaster will affect the sovereign bond haircut channel and destroy physical capital with the hypothesis that the disaster transmission runs through bond pricing, bank balance sheets, and through capital destruction as in \textcite{Gourio2012, Isore2017}. The consequences of this haircut for bank net worth and credit supply are discussed in Appendix \ref{sec:banking_block}. The disaster probability $\theta_t$ follows an exogenous log-AR(1) process specification as in \textcite{Isore2017}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:disaster_AR}
    \ln\theta_t = (1-\rho_\theta) \ln\bar\theta + \rho_\theta\ln\theta_{t-1} + \sigma_\theta\varepsilon_{\theta,t}, \qquad \varepsilon_{\theta,t} \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}
  \mathcal{N}(0,1)
\end{equation}
%
where $\bar{\theta}$ is the unconditional mean disaster probability, $\rho_\theta \in (0,1)$ is persistence, and $\sigma_\theta > 0$ the standard deviation of log-innovations. The log-AR(1) specification ensures $\theta_t > 0$ for all realisations and prevents the probability
from reaching the boundary values $\{0, 1\}$. Crucially, the disaster realization is a one-period event, meaning $\theta_t = Pr(x_{t+1}=1)$. At the beginning of period $t$, agents observe $\theta_t$, then the disaster draw $x_t$ is realised and production, trade, and market clearing will follow. If $x_{t+1} = 1$, a fresh draw $x_{t+2}$ is made from $\theta_{t+1}$ in the following period. The economy can therefore enter and exit the disaster state, which distinguishes the specification from absorbing-state formulations such as \textcite{Brede2013}.

In the baseline specification, the productivity innovation is switched
off, $\varepsilon_{z,t}=0$, so that the trend growth rate is deterministic
at $\mu$ and the only aggregate stochastic driver is the disaster
probability $\theta_t$ (with the realised indicator $x_t$). This isolates
the sovereign-disaster channel that is the object of study; a
conventional total-factor-productivity shock can be reintroduced as a
robustness exercise without altering the stationarised system, since
$\varepsilon_{z,t}$ enters only through the growth factor
$\Gamma_t=e^{x_t\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t}}$ already carried
throughout Appendix~\ref{sec:Stationarization}.
 
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} $\theta_t$ is bounded and dimensionless; no detrending required. The process passes directly into the stationarized system in Appendix~B. $\to$ Appendix~B.

%The state probabilities $\theta_t(s)$ such that $\sum_{s\in S}\theta(s)=1$ are time-varying and universally known to all agents, upon which they make an investment decision at time $t$.

%Thus, if $x_{t+1} = 1$ in period $t+1$, the economy is not trapped, therefore $x_{t+2}$ will result in a fresh draw of the disaster state. The \textcite{Gourio2012} stationarization trick will analytically eliminate $x_{t+1}$ from the equilibrium system, replacing it with $\theta_t$ in the detrended equations, which will make the perturbation methods feasible despite the large nonlinearity.


\subsection{Households}

The economy is populated by a representative household who derives utility from consumption $C_t$ and leisure $1-L_t$, supplies labour to intermediate-goods firms, owns the monopolistically competitive firms and the capital-goods producer, deposits savings with commercial banks, and pays lump-sum taxes. The household maximises the following inter-temporal objective with Epstein-Zin recursive preferences:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EZW}
  \tilde{V}_t = \Biggl[
    \bigl[C_t(1-L_t)^{\varpi}\bigr]^{1-\psi}
    +\,\beta_0
    \Bigl(\mathbb{E}_t\bigl[\tilde{V}_{t+1}^{1-\gamma}\bigr]\Bigr)^{\!
      \frac{1-\psi}{1-\gamma}}
  \Biggr]^{\!\frac{1}{1-\psi}}
\end{equation}
%
where $\beta_0\in(0,1)$ is the subjective discount factor, $\mathbb{E}_t$ the conditional expectations operator, $\psi>0$ the inverse of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution (\textsc{EIS}\,$=1/\psi$), $\gamma>0$ the coefficient of relative risk aversion (\textsc{rra}), and $\varpi>0$ the leisure weight in the consumption-leisure bundle.  Crucially, the Epstein-Zin formulation allows $\gamma\neq\psi$, decoupling risk aversion from the \textsc{ies}; when $\gamma=\psi$ the recursion collapses to standard expected utility. 

The household's budget constraint is adapted from the standard disaster-risk literature.  Households own capital producers and deposit with commercial banks rather than holding sovereign bonds directly; accordingly, no bond-return term appears in the flow constraint, but the dividends of the capital goods producers enter as an additional income term

\begin{equation}\label{eq:budget}
C_t + T_t + D_t
\;=\;
W^{\mathrm{real}}_t\,L_t 
 + R^d_{t-1}\,D_{t-1} + \Pi_t^f+ \Pi_t^{cp}
\end{equation}
%
where $D_t$ are real bank deposits earning gross rate $R^d_t$ next period, $T_t$ lump-sum taxes, $W^{\mathrm{real}}_t\equiv W_t/p_t$ the real wage, $\Pi_t^f$ dividends from monopolistic firms and dividens from capital producers. 

The capital-goods producer are a competitive, household-owned pass-through but not preference-bearing agent with a technology of new installed capital delivered per unit final good is discounted by the adjustment cost, 

\begin{equation}
    \Pi_t^{cp} = Q_t \left[I_t - \frac{\tau}{2}K_t\left(\frac{I_t}{K_t} - \frac{\bar{I}}{\bar{K}}\right)^2 \right] - I_t
\end{equation}

$K_t$ enters only as a scale variable (the aggregate installed capital stock, taken as given by the producer, and owned by entrepreneurs — see Appendix~\ref{sec:banking_block}). The producer is not preference-bearing and solves a static problem each period, so no Euler equation or new state variable is introduced. Maximising the capital good's producers profit over $I_t$ yields
%
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial {\Pi^{cp}}}{\partial I_t} = 0:\quad Q_t\left[1-\tau\left(\frac{I_t}{K_t}-\frac{\bar I}{\bar K}\right)\right]=1
\end{equation*}
%
which gives Tobin's $Q$:
%
\begin{equation}
  Q_t = \frac{1}{1-\tau\!\left(\dfrac{I_t}{K_t}-\dfrac{\bar{I}}{\bar{K}}\right)}
  \tag{A.1.4}\label{eq:CP_TobinsQ}
\end{equation}
%

The household chooses the sequence $\{C_t,L_t,D_t\}_{t=0}^{\infty}$ to maximise utility \eqref{eq:EZW} subject to the budget constraint \eqref{eq:budget}, absent any direct sovereign-bond exposure.  First-order optimality conditions are derived in Appendix~\ref{AppendixA}. We obtain their final results as
%
\begin{align}
     Q_{t,t+1} &= \beta_0
      \left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{\!-\psi}
      \!\!\left(\frac{1-L_{t+1}}{1-L_t}\right)^{\!\varpi(1-\psi)}
      \frac{V_{t+1}^{-\chi}}
           {\bigl(\mathbb{E}_t V_{t+1}^{1-\chi}\bigr)^{\frac{-\chi}{1-\chi}}} \tag{A.1.1}\label{eq:SDF} \\[6mm]
    C_t &= \frac{W^{real}_t(1-L_t)}{\varpi} \tag{A.1.2}\label{eq:HHO_C} \\[6mm]
  1 &= E_t[Q_{t,t+1}R_t^d] \tag{A.1.3}\label{eq:HHO_Deposit_Euler}
\end{align}
%
\paragraph{Endogenous Discount Factor $\beta(\theta_t)$.}
Following \textcite{Isore2017}, we integrate the disaster realisation $x_{t+1}$ out of the certainty equivalent $\mathcal{R}_t$ directly and using the Gourio (2012) scaling $\tilde V_{t+1}\big|_{x_{t+1}=1}=(1-\Delta^k)\,\tilde V_{t+1}\big|_{x_{t+1}=0}$ together with the symmetric productivity disaster\footnote{Productivity $z_t$ also contracts by $(1-\Delta^k)$ in the disaster state, following \textcite{Isore2017}.}, the binary indicator $x_{t+1}$ is eliminated through the \textcite{Gourio2012} trick in the detrended equilibrium system. The cost of this elimination is that the pseudo-discount factor $\beta(\theta_t)$ becomes time-varying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:betatheta}
    \beta(\theta_t)
    \;=\;
    \beta_0
    \Bigl[(1-\theta_t)+\theta_t(1-\Delta^k)^{1-\gamma}\Bigr]^{(1-\psi)/(1-\gamma)} \tag{A.1.5}
\end{equation}
At $\Delta^k=0$ or $\theta_t=0$ this reduces to $\beta_0$. For $\Delta^k>0$ and $\theta_t>0$, $\beta(\theta_t)$ varies with the disaster probability. When $\theta_t$ rises, the pseudo-discount factor shifts, producing an endogenous preference shock in the sense of \textcite{Isore2017}. The detrended Bellman, SDF, and capital-return equations (Appendix~\ref{sec:detrending}) carry $\beta(\theta_t)$ in place of $\beta_0$. This derivation is purely a property of the household's recursive preferences and is unaffected by which agent holds physical capital. The elimination applies to the certainty equivalent specifically. The indicator $x_{t+1}$ survives elsewhere in the household block, in particular in the trend factor $\Gamma_{t+1}$ carried by the stochastic discount factor itself and is integrated out separately wherever it appears; $\beta(\theta_t)$ is therefore not a universal substitute for the Gourio operation but the result of applying it at one specific exponent, $\kappa=1-\gamma$.

\paragraph{Risk-Free Rate and Deposit Market.}
While \textcite{Isore2017} identify the risk-free rate through a household bond Euler $\mathbb{E}_t[Q_{t,t+1}\frac{(1+r_t)}{(1+\pi_{t+1})}]=1$, households in this model hold no sovereign bonds, so \eqref{eq:HHO_Deposit_Euler} replaces that equation on the household side. The risk-free rate is defined as
%
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:riskfree_rate}
  R^f_t \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_t[Q_{t,t+1}]} \tag{A.1.6}
\end{equation*}
%
from the SDF directly, independently of market clearing. The deposit market clearing condition $R^d_t = R^f_t$, then ensures the deposit rate equals the risk-free rate in equilibrium, closing the household's asset pricing block. 

Because households save exclusively through bank deposits and banks never default in this model, $R^f_t$ as defined in~\eqref{eq:riskfree_rate} is a genuinely state-independent rate, so the return on a claim paying one unit in every realisation of $x_{t+1}$. This is a structural consequence of moving sovereign bonds onto bank balance sheets ($\phi>0$). While in \textcite{Isore2017}, the household's saving vehicle is the sovereign bond itself, which is state-contingent whenever $\Delta^b>0$; the object their bond Euler prices is therefore not the same object as~\eqref{eq:riskfree_rate}.  The consequence is that $\mathbb{E}_t[Q_{t,t+1}]$ must be evaluated explicitly rather than replaced by its no-disaster realisation, which is done in equation~\eqref{eq:B_EQ} below.


\subsection{Firms} \label{sec:firms_and_entrepreneurs}

The economy has final and intermediate good producers. The first faces a maximisation problem of final aggregated goods under monopolistic competition, while the latter first minimizes costs to maximize profits. Calculations for each optimisation step can be found in Appendix~\ref{appsec:firms}


 \subsubsection{Final Good Production}
 
Following \textcite{Isore2017} in the origins of \textcite{DixitStiglitz1977}, we assume that a representative final-goods firm aggregates intermediate varieties $Y_{j,t}$ into final output in monopolistic competition 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aggregator}
  Y_t = \left(\int_0^1 Y_{j,t}^{(\nu-1)/\nu}\,dj\right)^{\nu/(\nu-1)} \tag{A.2.1}
\end{equation}
%
The profit profit maximisation gives demand for variety $j$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:demand}
  Y_{j,t} = Y_t\left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu} \tag{A.2.2}
\end{equation}
%
Since we can constitute that the final good is the aggregation of prices times their intermediate quantities, we can write the aggregate price index as 

\begin{align*}
    p_t = \Big(\int_0^1 p_{j,t}^{1-\nu} dj\Big)^\frac{1}{1-\nu}
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Intermediate Good Production} \label{sec:int_good}

The production of an intermediate good follows a two-step process. First, the firm minimizes costs to obtain aggregated real tems for wages and the rental rate of capital. Thenafter, firms are maximising their profits, subject to the demand schedule derived in the final good production, making the process backward-inducted. 


\paragraph{Cost Minimisation} Before final goods can be aggregated, intermediate good producers use labor and capital as production inputs. To minimise production costs, they choose optimal factor quantities in each given time period. Therefore, we assume that these firms take the wage $W_t$, the rental rate of capital $P_t^k$ as well as nominal factor prices as given. The underlying production function requires these firms produce at least as much of the intermediate good as it is demanded at a given price. The intra-temporal intermediate firm problem results in 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost_minimisation_int}
    \min_{L_{j,t}, \tilde{K}_{j,t}} W_{t}L_{j,t} + P_t^k\tilde{K}_{j,t} \qquad \text{s.t.} \quad \tilde{K}^\alpha_{j,t}(z_tL_{j,t})^{1-\alpha} \geq \left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu}Y_t \tag{A.2.3}
\end{equation}
%
where $z_t$ denotes the level of productivity and $\tilde{K}_{j,t} \equiv u_tK_{j,t}$ expresses effective capital. Their first order solutions are derived in Appendix \ref{appsec:firms}. Hence, the aggregated real term optimality conditions are 
%
\begin{equation*}
    w_t = mc^*_t(1-\alpha)z_t^{1-\alpha}\tilde{K}_t^\alpha L_t^{-\alpha} \tag{A.2.4}
\end{equation*}
%
and
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:resale_price}
    P_t^{k, real} = mc^*_t\cdot\alpha\cdot z^{1-\alpha}\tilde{K}_t^{\alpha-1} L_t^{1-\alpha}   \tag{A.2.5}
\end{equation*}
%
As we further follow \textcite{Gourio2012} for the disaster process, we assume that productivity grows at 

\begin{equation} \label{eq:prod_process}
    \frac{z_{t+1}}{z_t} = e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\epsilon_{z,t+1}} \tag{A.2.7}
\end{equation}
% 
\paragraph{Profit Maximisation}
We further assume price setting as in \textcite{Calvo1983}, in which each intermediate good producer may reset its price with probability $1-\zeta$ in any given period, while with  probability $\zeta$ it keeps its price unchanged. A firm that resets its price  $p_{j,t}$ at time $t$ chooses it to maximise the present discounted value of  profits over all future periods in which the price may remain fixed, subject to the demand schedule derived from \eqref{eq:aggregator} holds in every future period 
$t+s$ in which $p_{j,t}$ remains fixed:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calvo_max}
    \max_{p_{j,t}}\; E_t \sum_{s=0}^\infty (\zeta)^s Q_{t, t+s}
    \left(p_{j,t}Y_{j,t+s} - W_{t+s}L_{j,t+s} - P^k_{t+s}\tilde{K}_{j,t+s}
    \right) \tag{A.2.8a}
\end{equation}
%
where $Q_{t, t+s} \equiv \Lambda_{t+s}^B/\Lambda_t^B$ is the stochastic discount factor inherited from the household problem, with $Q_t = 1$.
%
The ratio between recent price and current price required to derive the inflation rate ratio is derived in a maximisation Appendix \ref{appsec:profitmax}. Defining the gross reset inflation rate as $1+\pi_t^* \equiv p_t^*/p_{t-1}$  and the gross inflation rate as $1+\pi_t \equiv p_t/p_{t-1}$, such that $p_t^*/p_t = (1+\pi_t^*)/(1+\pi_t)$, the ratio above can be written recursively by introducing auxiliary variables $\Xi_{1_t}$ and $\Xi_{2_t}$:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reset_inflation}\tag{A.2.9}
    1 + \pi_t^* = \frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\frac{\Xi_{1_t}}{\Xi_{2_t}}(1+\pi_t)
\end{equation}
%
where the recursive auxiliary variables are defined as
%
\begin{align}
    \Xi_{1_t} &= Y_t mc_t^* + \zeta\, E_t\!\left[Q_{t,t+1}
    (1+\pi_{t+1})^\nu \,\Xi_{1_{t+1}}\right] \label{eq:aux1}\tag{A.2.10}\\[4pt]
    \Xi_{2_t} &= Y_t + \zeta\, E_t\!\left[Q_{t,t+1}
    (1+\pi_{t+1})^{\nu-1}\,\Xi_{2_{t+1}}\right] \label{eq:aux2}\tag{A.2.11}
\end{align}
%
with $mc_t^*$ the real marginal cost of production at time $t$.

\paragraph{Aggregated Inflation and Production} The aggregated price index of the economy consists of a share of firms $1-\zeta$ that are not able to reset their pricing levels of at given time $t$ and its remainder fraction $\zeta$ of the previous period price expressed as

\begin{equation*}
    p_t^{1-\nu}= (1-\zeta)p_t^{*1-\nu} + \zeta p_{t-1}^{1-\nu}  = \int_0^{1-\zeta} p_t^{*1-\nu}dj + \int_{1-\zeta}^1 p_{j,t-1}^{1-\nu}
\end{equation*}
%
so the inflation rate is expressed as 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:inflation_rate}
    (1+\pi_t)^{1-\nu} = (1-\zeta)(1+\pi_t^*)^{1-\nu} + \zeta \tag{A.2.12}
\end{equation}
%
This is helpful for determining the aggregate production function as we can make use of the budget constraint from the cost minimisation of intermediate firms in (\ref{eq:cost_minimisation_int}) and aggregating aross intermediate firms. The derivation can be found in Appendix~\ref{appsec:agginflation}. The resulting aggregate production function 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:agg_production}
Y_t = \frac{\tilde{K_t}^\alpha(z_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}}{\int_0^1(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t})^{-\nu}} = \frac{\tilde{K_t}^\alpha(z_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}}{\Omega} \tag{A.2.13}
\end{equation}
%
is leveled with $\Omega$ as price distortion measure due to relative dispersion in relative prices from sticky prices. With the Calvo-Pricing assumption, the inflation rate can be written in aggregated terms of $\Omega$:

\begin{align*}\label{eq:omega}
    \Omega =& \int_0^1\left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu} dj = (1-\zeta)\left(\frac{p_t^*}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu} + p_t^\nu\int_{1-\zeta}^1p_{{j,t-1}}^{-\nu} dj = p_t^{\nu}\int_0^1 p_t^{-\nu} \\[3mm]
    =& \quad (1-\zeta)\left(\frac{1+\pi_t}{1+\pi^*_t}\right)^{\nu} + \zeta(1+\pi_t)^{\nu}\Omega_{t-1} \tag{A.2.14}
\end{align*}


\subsection{Entrepreneurs and Banking Sector}

This section extends the developments of \textcite{Isore2017} by integrating an entrepreneur à la \textcite{Bernanke1999} into a NK DSGE setting with financial frictions, which connects the household and firm sector with the banking sector developed from \textcite{Gertler2011}. 

\subsubsection{Entrepreneurs}

The economy consists of a continuum of identical, risk-neutral entrepreneurs of mass one. Each purchase $K_{t+1}$ units of installed capital at the end of period~$t$ from the capital goods producer (Appendix~\ref{sec:households}) at price $Q_t$ and rents capital to intermediate goods firms in period $t=1$ at the real rental rate $P^k_{t+1}$. capital that is retained at the same period is valued at the same price $Q_t$ that applies to newly purchased units. The framing of buying and selling capital used throughout this section is a standard accounting convention in this literature and not a literal transaction. A continuing entrepreneur need not physically dispose of capital it retains, since resale and purchase occur at the same price. Each entrepreneur has net worth $N^e_t$ and finances the gap $Q_tK_{t+1} - N^e_t$ by borrowing from a bank at gross real loan rate $R^S_t$, contracted at end of period~$t$.

An idiosyncratic productivity $\omega_{j,t+1}$ is drawn i.i.d.\ with $\mathbb{E}[\omega] = 1$ and c.d.f.\ $F(\cdot)$. The bank cannot observe $\omega_{j,t+1}$ without paying a monitoring cost $\mu \in (0,1)$ of the entrepreneur's gross revenue, which is the costly state verification (CSV) problem of \textcite{Townsend1979}. The optimal contract specifies a default threshold $\bar{\omega}_t$ such that entrepreneur $j$ defaults if and only if $\omega_{j,t+1} < \bar{\omega}_t$. Because entrepreneurs are risk-neutral, they absorb aggregate risk and the participation constraint holds state-by-state after aggregate shocks to $R^K_{t+1}$ realise \parencite[Section~III]{Bernanke1999}.We assume the reduced-form external finance premium as derived in \textcite[pp. 1352]{Bernanke1999} as given.  Consequently, $\bar\omega_t$ does not appear as a separate endogenous variable below.

$K_{t+1}$ here denotes the quantity committed to at the end of period $t$, before $x_{t+1}$ is reaslized while the capital law of motion's $K_{t+1}$ denotes the  the same object after the disaster realization, related by the Gourio factor.
Given capital $K_{t+1}$ acquired at $t$, the entrepreneur chooses period-$(t{+}1)$ utilisation $u_{t+1}$ to maximise gross revenue from the capital stock. Doing so takes as given the rental price $P^{k,real}_{t+1}$ as derived in the intermediate good prodcuers cost minimisation resulting in equation~\ref{eq:resale_price} and the resale price $Q_{t+1}$ set by the capital-goods producer (Equation~\ref{eq:CP_TobinsQ}). Since the disaster factor $e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)}$ and the capital stock $K_{t+1}$ scale total revenue multiplicatively and do not depend on $u_{t+1}$, maximising realised revenue (for any realisation of $x_{t+1}$ and any idiosyncratic draw $\omega_{j,t+1}$) is equivalent to maximising per-unit revenue:
%
\begin{equation*}
  \max_{u_{t+1}}\;\;   P_{t+1}^{k, real}\,u_{t+1} + Q_{t+1}\bigl(1-\delta_0 u_{t+1}^{\eta}\bigr)
\end{equation*}
%
This objective is well-defined independently of the debt contract's default threshold $\bar\omega_t$. Therefore, utilisation maximises the total surplus available for division between entrepreneur and bank, not the entrepreneur's own share of it, so the choice is unaffected by contract details. The first-order condition is derived as 
%
\begin{equation}
  P_{t+1}^{k, real}  = Q_{t+1}\,\delta_0\,\eta\,u_{t+1}^{\eta-1}
  \tag{A.3.1.1}\label{eq:A3_util}
\end{equation}
%
with second-order condition $-Q_{t+1}\delta_0\eta(\eta-1)u_{t+1}^{\eta-2}<0$, showing that the standard convexity assumption on the depreciation function is satisfied for $\eta>1$ which has been already imposed throughout the model. 

\paragraph{Consistency with the Capital-Goods Producer.}
The two margins that \textcite{Isore2017}'s household jointly optimises are now split across two agents, but both continue to price the same
market-clearing capital good. The capital-goods producer's investment condition~\eqref{eq:CP_TobinsQ}, initiated at date $t+1$, and the
entrepreneur's utilisation condition~\eqref{eq:A3_util} both determine the single date-$(t{+}1)$ price $Q_{t+1}$:
%
The capital-goods producer's investment condition~\eqref{eq:CP_TobinsQ}, instantiated at date $t+1$, and the entrepreneur's utilisation condition~\eqref{eq:A3_util} both determine the single date-$(t{+}1)$ price $Q_{t+1}$:
%
\begin{equation*}
  Q_{t+1}
  \;=\;\frac{1}{1-\tau\!\left(\dfrac{I_{t+1}}{K_{t+1}}-\dfrac{\bar{I}}{\bar{K}}\right)}
  \;=\;\frac{P^{k,\mathrm{real}}_{t+1}}{\delta_0\,\eta\,u_{t+1}^{\eta-1}}.
\end{equation*}
%
Eliminating $Q_{t+1}$ between the two recovers exactly \textcite{Isore2017}'s single combined household condition:
%
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{1-\tau\!\left(\dfrac{I_{t+1}}{K_{t+1}}-\dfrac{\bar{I}}{\bar{K}}\right)} = \frac{P^{k,\mathrm{real}}_{t+1}}{\delta_0\,\eta\; u_{t+1}^{\,\eta-1}}
\end{equation*}
%
confirming that the two-agent decomposition is algebraically equivalent to the original joint FOC.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Return on Capital}
\label{par:return_on_capital}
 
Evaluated at the optimal utilisation \eqref{eq:A3_util}, the gross real return to holding one unit of capital from $t$ to $t+1$ is
 
\begin{equation}
  R^K_{t+1}
  = e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)}\,
    \frac{P^{k, real}_{t+1}\, u_{t+1}
          + Q_{t+1}\bigl(1 - \delta_0\, u_{t+1}^{\eta}\bigr)}
         {Q_t} \tag{A.3.1.2}
  \label{eq:A3_RK}
\end{equation}
%
where $u_{t+1}$ solves \eqref{eq:A3_util} and $\delta_{t+1} = \delta_0\, u_{t+1}^{\eta}$ is the resulting utilisation-dependent depreciation rate.
 
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} $R^K_{t+1}$ is a ratio of detrended prices and is already scale-invariant. No further detrending required. $\to$ Appendix~B.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{External Finance Premium.}
\label{par:A313}

The CSV contracting problem yields a loan contract under which the expected return the entrepreneur must generate on capital exceeds the loan rate it is charged, by a wedge that is decreasing in the entrepreneur's equity share in total capital.  This wedge is the external finance premium; it is distinct from, and additional to, the bank's own margin $R^S_t-R^d_t$, which is generated by the leverage constraint of Appendix~\ref{sec:A3.2} rather than by the contracting
problem.  We use the reduced form of \textcite{Bernanke1999}:

% A.3.1.3 -- REVISED: f(.) is the CSV premium schedule (definition of the
%            function), NOT a statement that the bank spread depends on
%            entrepreneur leverage. The premium enters the participation
%            condition A.3.1.6.
\begin{equation}
  f\!\left(\frac{N^e_t}{Q_t K_{t+1}}\right)
  \;\equiv\; f_0
    \left(\frac{N^e_t}{Q_t K_{t+1}}\right)^{\!-\chi^e},
  \qquad \chi^e > 0,\; f_0 > 0
  \label{eq:A3_EFP} \tag{A.3.1.3}
\end{equation}
%
where $N^e_t / Q_t K_{t+1}$ is the entrepreneur's equity share in total capital, with $Q_t$ the capital producer's price. When $N^e_t$ falls, the premium widens, amplifying the initial shock through the financial accelerator. The parameter $f_0$ is back-solved from a target target
steady-state entrepreneur premium $\mathbb{E}[\bar R^K]/\bar R^S$ given a target equity ratio $\bar N^e/\bar Q\bar K^{n}$, following \textcite{Christiano2014}; the closed form is $f_0=\mathcal{F}\,\varkappa^{\chi^e}$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:D7}).  The bank margin $\bar R^S/\bar R^d$ is a separate calibration target, matched through the leverage multiplier $\lambda$ rather than through $f_0$.\footnote{The reduced form is a local approximation around the calibrated equity ratio and is not normalised so that $f(1)=1$; at the calibration used here $f_0<1$, so the schedule should not be extrapolated to equity ratios near unity.}

% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} $N^e_t / Q_t K_{t+1}$ is a ratio of real quantities both growing at trend rate $Z_t$; the ratio is stationary after detrending by $Z_t$. Full stationarized form in Appendix~B. $\to$ Appendix~B.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Entrepreneur Net Worth Accumulation.}
\label{par:A314}

Following further \textcite{Bernanke1999}, a fraction $\sigma^e \in (0,1)$ of entrepreneurs survive each period and carry their net worth forward. The exiting fraction $(1-\sigma^e)$ transfers net worth to households as a lump sum. A startup transfer $\iota^e$ from households to entering entrepreneurs prevents
the entrepreneurs net worth to tend towards a value of zero:

\begin{equation}
  N^e_t
  = \sigma^e \!\left[
      \underbrace{R^K_t \cdot Q_{t-1}K_t}_{\text{gross return on capital}}
      - R^S_{t-1} \cdot \underbrace{\bigl(Q_{t-1}K_t - N^e_{t-1}\bigr)}_{\text{interest on externally financed capital}}
    \right]
    + \iota^e \tag{A.3.1.4}
  \label{eq:A3_NE}
\end{equation}
% 
In the standard \textcite{Bernanke1999} model without a banking sector, the lender's opportunity cost is the risk-free rate, so equation~\eqref{eq:A3_EFP} places the premium between $\mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}]$ and $R^f_t$ directly.  Here the lender is a constrained bank whose required return on the loan book is $R^S_t>R^d_t$, determined by the binding leverage constraint~\eqref{eq:A323} together with loan-market clearing (A.5.4).  The premium of~\eqref{eq:A3_EFP} then applies on top of $R^S_t$ rather than on top of $R^f_t$, which is the sense in which the two frictions stack.

% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} $R^K_t \cdot Q_{t-1}K_t$ has dimension $Z_{t-1}$. Divide through by $Z_{t-1}$ and apply the Gourio-trick growth normalization. Full stationarized form: $\hat{n}^e_t = \sigma^e e^{-\mu}[\cdots] + \hat{\iota}^e$. $\to$ Appendix~B.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Entrepreneur Balance Sheet Identity}
\label{par:A315}

The value to entrepreneurs equals the funding gap:

\begin{equation}
  Q_tS_t \;\equiv\; Q_t K_{t+1} - N^e_t \tag{A.3.1.5}
  \label{eq:A3_entrep_BS}
\end{equation}
%
which constitutes an accounting identity, defining the quantity $Q_tS_t$ that enters the bank balance sheet equation~\eqref{eq:A3_bankBS} and the leverage constraint equation~\eqref{eq:A323}.

% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} Divide through by $Z_t$. All three terms detrend directly. $\to$ Appendix~B.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Capital Demand: Free-Entry Condition}
\label{par:capital_market_clearing}
 
Financing of $K_{t+1}$ is decided at the end of period $t$, taking $Q_t$ (capital-goods producer, Appendix~\ref{sec:households}), the loan rate $R^S_t$, and the premium schedule~\eqref{eq:A3_EFP} as given.  An individual entrepreneur is atomistic relative to the continuum and therefore treats both $R^S_t$ and the aggregate equity ratio entering $f(\cdot)$ parametrically: it does not perceive its own $K_{t+1}$ choice as moving either. Expected end-of-period profit is then
%
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}]\,Q_tK_{t+1} - R^S_t\bigl(Q_tK_{t+1}-N^e_t\bigr)
  \;=\;
  \bigl(\mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}]-R^S_t\bigr)Q_tK_{t+1} + R^S_tN^e_t,
\end{equation*}
%
which is linear in $K_{t+1}$. A determinate, finite, positive level of capital demand is therefore an equilibrium only if the coefficient on $K_{t+1}$ vanishes: at any strictly positive coefficient the atomistic entrepreneur prefers $K_{t+1}\to\infty$, viceversa at any strictly negative coefficient it prefers $K_{t+1}=0$. Following \textcite{Gertler2011}, free entry and competitive credit markets impose exactly that 

%
% A.3.1.6 -- the participation / free-entry condition, where f(.) enters:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[R^K_{t+1}\right]
  = f\!\left(\frac{N_t^e}{Q_tK_{t+1}}\right) R^S_t
  \label{eq:A3_indiff} \tag{A.3.1.6}
\end{equation}
\textcite{Isore2017} impose the household capital Euler $\mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}] = R^f_t$ directly. In this model, entrepreneurs finance capital with bank loans, so the relevant no-arbitrage condition is against the loan rate, with $\mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}] > R^S_t \geq R^d_t = R^f_t$. Equation~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff} replaces their capital Euler and is the equation that connects the firm sector \ref{appsec:firms} to the banking sector \ref{sec:A3.2}. 

The rate wedge is the external finance premium derived in equation~\eqref{eq:A3_EFP}. Equation~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff} is a condition that $R^S_t$ must satisfy; it does not by itself determine $R^S_t$.  The equilibrium loan rate is pinned by the balance sheet chain rather than by ~~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff} itself. Given the development of bank net worth $N^b_t$, the binding leverage constraint and the home-bias identity in the next susection, loan supply is fixed at $Q_tS_t=(1-\phi)\lambda N^b_t$. The entrepreneur balance sheet~\eqref{eq:A3_entrep_BS} then fixes $Q_tK_{t+1}=Q_tS_t+N^e_t$ and hence the equity ratio entering $f(\cdot)$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff} returns the $R^S_t$ consistent with that quantity. Loan-market clearing~\eqref{eq:loanmarketclearing} is the statement that these two routes to $Q_tS_t$ agree; it is a consequence of the optimality conditions, not an addition to them. This is the channel through which a sovereign loss on bank balance sheets raises the cost of credit to entrepreneurs.
 
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} Both sides are gross rates --- scale-invariant. $\to$ Appendix~B.

\subsubsection{Banks with Sovereign Bonds}
\label{sec:A3.2}

A continuum of identical banks of mass one each raise deposits $D_{j,t}$ from households at gross real deposit rate $R^d_t$, accumulate net worth
$N^b_{j,t}$ from retained earnings, and hold two asset classes: loans $L_{j,t}$ to entrepreneurs at gross real loan rate $R^S_t$, and domestic sovereign bonds $B^b_{j,t}$ earning gross real return $R^b_t$. The portfolio share of sovereign bonds in total bank assets is fixed by the home-bias parameter $\phi \in [0,1)$.

Banks are owned by households. To motivate an endogenous constraint on bank leverage, \textcite{Gertler2011} introduce a moral hazard friction: at the start of each period, a banker can divert a fraction $\theta^b$ of managed assets and transfer them to the owning household as private gain. Depositors lend only if the continuation value of operating exceeds the gain from diverting; this incentive compatibility condition becomes the leverage constraint in equation~\eqref{eq:A323}.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Bank Balance Sheet Identity}
\label{par:A321}

\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\,Q_t S_{j,t}}_{\text{loans}}
  + \underbrace{\,Q^b_t B^b_{j,t}}_{\text{sovereign bonds}}
  =
  \underbrace{N^b_{j,t}}_{\text{net worth}}
  + \underbrace{D_{j,t}}_{\text{deposits}} \tag{A.3.2.1}
  \label{eq:A3_bankBS}
\end{equation}
%
The home-bias parameter $\phi$ splits bank assets between loans and sovereign bonds. When $\phi = 0$ the model collapses to the standard \textcite{Gertler2011} specification with loans only. Bankers then maximise continuation value subject to a moral-hazard incentive constraint. This comes as both the value function and the constraint are linear in net worth and assets (proved in Appendix, eq. \eqref{eq:Banker_Value}).

\paragraph{Banker's Leverage Constraint} Bankers expand assets to the limit whenever $\eta_t>0$ and therefore the franchise value of asset holdings is positive relative to the outside option of
depositing at $R^d_t$, so the constraint binds with equality in equilibrium. This gives the individual bank's leverage constraint that reduces to a leverage cap.
 
\begin{equation}\tag{A.3.2.3}\label{eq:A323}
    \,Q_t S_{j,t}+Q^b_t B^b_{j,t}
    \;\leq\;
    \lambda_t\,N^b_{j,t}
\end{equation}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Home-Bias Portfolio Identity}
\label{par:A324}

In the euro-area institutional context, domestic banks are the predominant holders of domestic sovereign debt, reflecting the zero risk-weight treatment of home-country government bonds under Basel capital requirements, their eligibility as high-quality liquid assets and the structural incentives that systematically favour domestic over cross-border sovereign bond holdings \parencite{Gennaioli2014,Bocola2016}

The parameter $\phi \in [0,1)$ captures this as a structural characteristic of the bank balance sheet rather than a portfolio decision variable. Given these institutional frictions banks are effectively price-takers in the sovereign bond market at the composition margin, and $\phi$ is calibrated to the observed average domestic sovereign bond share in euro-area bank total assets. Formally, $\phi$ is defined as the bond share of total assets $A_{j,t}\equiv Q_tS_{j,t}+Q^b_tB^b_{j,t}$ (as prior defined in paragraph \ref{par:A322}): 
%
\begin{equation*}
  Q^b_t B^b_{j,t} = \phi\, A_{j,t}, \qquad Q_t S_{j,t} = (1-\phi)\, A_{j,t}.
\end{equation*}
%
Dividing the two gives the portfolio condition directly:
%
\begin{equation}
  Q^b_t B^b_t \;=\; \frac{\phi}{1-\phi}\,Q_tS_t
  \tag{A.3.2.4}\label{eq:A3_homebias}
\end{equation}
%
Combined with the entrepreneur balance sheet~\eqref{eq:A3_entrep_BS},
equation~\eqref{eq:A3_homebias} closes the bank asset side as $Q_tS_t$ is pinned by entrepreneur equity and the binding leverage constraint \eqref{eq:A323}, and $Q^b_t B^b_t$ is then determined proportionally. The counterfactual sets $\phi = 0$, which eliminates the sovereign bond portfolio to the standard \textcite{Gertler2011} specification with loans only, shutting down the sovereign-bank transmission channel while preserving the standard BGG financial accelerator.

\paragraph{Bank Net Worth Accumulation.}
\label{par:A325}

Aggregating across surviving bankers $\sigma^b$ and adding
startup transfers $\iota$ to newly entering bankers, the \textcite{Gertler2011} augmented bank net worth accumulation equation reads as

\begin{equation}
  N^b_t
  = \sigma^b
    \Bigl[
      (R^S_{t-1} - R^d_{t-1})\,Q_{t-1}S_{t-1}
      + (R^b_t - R^d_{t-1})\,\,Q^b_{t-1}B^b_{t-1}
      + R^d_{t-1}\,N^b_{t-1}
    \Bigr]
    + \iota \tag{A.3.2.5}
  \label{eq:A3_Bank_Value}
\end{equation}
%
Surviving bankers earn at first the excess return on loans $(R^S_{t-1} - R^d_t)$ on their loan portfolio, secondly the excess return on sovereign bonds $(R^b_t - R^d_t)$ on their bond portfolio and thirdly the deposit rate on their existing net worth. The startup transfer $\iota$ is calibrated residually following \textcite{Christiano2014} to match a target $\bar{N}^b/\bar{K}$ ratio.

% When $\theta_t$ rises, $Q^b_t$ falls (equation~\eqref{eq:A3_bondprice}), which reduces the mark-to-market value $Q^b_{t-1}B^b_{t-1}$ and compresses $R^b_t$ (equation~\eqref{eq:A3_Rb}). The second term in brackets in~\eqref{eq:A3_Bank_Value} therefore falls, eroding $N^b_t$ without any realised defaults, purely through the anticipated sovereign haircut. This is the sovereign-bank channel.

\paragraph{Equilibrium Bond Pricing Condition}
\label{par:bank_bond:euler}

With portfolio composition pinned by the structural parameter $\phi$ \eqref{eq:A323}, sovereign bonds are not a free choice variable for the individual bank: the quantity $Q^b_t B^b_{j,t}$ is determined residually once total assets $A_{j,t} = \lambda N^b_{j,t}$ and loan volume $Q_t S_{j,t}$ are fixed by the binding leverage constraint (\ref{eq:A323}). The sovereign bond price $Q^b_t$ is therefore an equilibrium price that adjusts endogenously to clear the bond market at the fixed composition ratio $\phi$.

Since bankers are owned by households, they use the household stochastic discount factor $\Lambda^B_{t,t+1}$ to discount future payoffs \parencite{Gertler2011}.
The bank's outside option is the deposit rate, which satisfies the household deposit Euler \eqref{eq:HHO_Deposit_Euler} at rate $R^f_t$. For banks to hold sovereign bonds willingly at the institutional share $\phi$, $Q^b_t$ must adjust until the bond return satisfies the same pricing condition under the household discount factor:
 
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\frac{\Lambda^B_{t,t+1}}{\Lambda^B_{t}}\, R^b_{t+1}\right] = 1
  \tag{A.3.3.1}
  \label{eq:A3_bondeuler}
\end{equation}
%
While in \textcite{Isore2017} bonds are held by households and therefore the household optimality condition incorporates sovereign bonds, we assume for the sake of isolating the sovereign-bank transmission channel cleanly that households do not hold sovereign bonds. Their budget constraint contains no bond term (see \ref{sec:households}). Equation~\eqref{eq:A3_bondeuler} is therefore derived from the bank's equilibrium pricing requirement, not the household's optimality condition. The pricing implication is identical because the bank uses the household SDF $Q_{t,t+1}$ as its discount factor.

Equation~\eqref{eq:A3_bondeuler} is the equilibrium bond pricing condition for which $Q^b_t$ adjusts endogenously until it holds. It takes the standard asset pricing form $\mathbb{E}_t[Q_{t,t+1} R^i_{t+1}] = 1$ for asset~$i$, identical in structure to \textcite{Isore2017} but derived from the bank's willingness-to-hold condition rather than a portfolio first-order condition.

\paragraph{Asset-Specific Resilience.}
\label{par:asset_specific_resilience}
 
Following \textcite{Gabaix2012}, the linearity-generating process assigns each risky asset an asset-specific resilience that summarizes the disaster-state expected return loss. For the sovereign bond paying $(1 - \Delta^b)$ in the disaster state and $1$ in the BAU state we can state that
 
\begin{equation}
  H^b_t \;=\; 1 - \theta_t \cdot \Delta^b \cdot \tilde\Lambda^M
  \tag{A.3.3.2}
  \label{eq:A3_resil}
\end{equation}
%
where $\Delta^b \in (0,1)$ is the sovereign haircut in a disaster and
%
\begin{equation*}
  \tilde\Lambda^M_t
  \;\equiv\;
  \frac{\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[M_{t+1}\mid\text{disaster}\right]}
       {\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[M_{t+1}\right]}
\end{equation*}
%
is the disaster SDF loading, normalised by the unconditional conditional mean of the stochastic discount factor rather than by its no-disaster realisation.  The normalisation is what makes $H^b_t$ commensurate with the risk-free rate $R^f_t=1/\mathbb{E}_t[M_{t+1}]$ against which it is divided in~\eqref{eq:A3_bondprice}; without it the bond Euler~\eqref{eq:A3_bondeuler} would hold only to first order in $\theta_t$.  $H^b_t$ captures the disaster-probability-weighted reduction in the bond's risk-adjusted payoff.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Sovereign Bond Price}
\label{par:A333}

Following \textcite{Gabaix2012} for the closed-form expression for $Q^b_t$ via the linearity-generating process and \textcite{Isore2017} for implement their risky-bond extension implementation, we substitute the gross real return $R^b_{t+1} = (1 - x_{t+1}\Delta^b)/Q^b_t$ into the bond Euler~\eqref{eq:A3_bondeuler} and by taking expectations:

\begin{equation*}
  \frac{1}{Q^b_t}\,
  \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\Lambda^B_{t,t+1}
  \bigl(1 - x_{t+1}\Delta^b\bigr)\right] = 1
\end{equation*}
%
We can then apply the \textcite{Gabaix2012} decomposition, separating the two states of $x_{t+1}$ in the expectation and using
$\mathbb{E}_t[\Lambda^B_{t,t+1}]=1/R^f_t$,
%
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\Lambda^B_{t,t+1}\bigl(1-x_{t+1}\Delta^b\bigr)\right]
  &= \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\Lambda^B_{t,t+1}\right]
     -\Delta^b\,\theta_t\,M^{d}_{t+1} \\[3pt]
  &= \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\Lambda^B_{t,t+1}\right]
     \left(1-\Delta^b\,\theta_t\,
     \frac{M^{d}_{t+1}}{\mathbb{E}_t[\Lambda^B_{t,t+1}]}\right)
   = \frac{1-\theta_t\,\Delta^b\,\tilde\Lambda^M_t}{R^f_t}
   = \frac{H^b_t}{R^f_t}
   \tag{A.3.3.3}
   \label{eq:A3_bondprice}
\end{align*}
%
which holds exactly.
%
% \parencite[Appendix~A.8, Eq.~A.40]{Isore2017};
% \parencite[Eq.~4]{Gabaix2012}.
% 

% \textcite{Isore2017} allow capital destruction ($\Delta^k > 0$),  which modifies $\beta(\theta_t)$ and introduces a disaster factor in the household capital Euler.
 
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} $Q^b_t$ is a ratio of dimensionless objects; $R^f_t$ is a gross rate. Both are scale-invariant. $\to$ Appendix~B.
 
% \medskip
% \noindent\textit{Dynare implementation note:}
% Equation~\eqref{eq:A3_bondprice} is used directly in Dynare as a closed-form expression in place of iterating on the full bond Euler. It is exact at the non-stochastic steady state and correct to first order in perturbation. At third order, small deviations from the full bond Euler may arise; these are accepted as the cost of preserving tractability, consistent with the approach of \textcite{Isore2017} in their risky-bond extension.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Sovereign Bond Gross Return}
\label{par:A334}
 
The one-period gross real return on the sovereign bond, accounting
for the disaster haircut:
 
\begin{equation}
  R^b_{t+1}
  = \frac{1 - x_{t+1}\,\Delta^b}{Q^b_t}
  \tag{A.3.3.4}
  \label{eq:A3_Rb}
\end{equation}
%
where $x_{t+1} \in \{0,1\}$ is the disaster realisation at the
start of period $t+1$, with $R^b_{t+1} = 1/Q^b_t$ in the BAU state and $R^b_{t+1} = (1-\Delta^b)/Q^b_t < 1/Q^b_t$ in the disaster state.
%  
Taking the conditional expectation of equation~\eqref{eq:A3_Rb} and substituting
equation~\eqref{eq:A3_bondprice}, we arrive at the expected sovereign bond gross return:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[R^b_{t+1}\right]
  = \frac{1 - \theta_t\,\Delta^b}{Q^b_t}
  = \frac{(1 - \theta_t\,\Delta^b)\,R^f_t}
         {1 - \theta_t\,\Delta^b\,\tilde\Lambda^M}
  \tag{A.3.3.5}
  \label{eq:A3_ERb}
\end{equation}
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} $R^b_{t+1}$ is a gross rate --- scale-invariant. $\to$ Appendix~B.
The sovereign spread is defined as the excess of the bond's promised gross yield over the risk-free rate,
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{spread}_t
  \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{Q^b_t} - R^f_t
  \;=\; R^f_t\,\frac{\theta_t\,\Delta^b\,\tilde\Lambda^M_t}{H^b_t}
  \;\approx\; \theta_t \cdot \Delta^b \cdot \tilde\Lambda^M_t
  \tag{A.3.3.6}
  \label{eq:A3_spread}
\end{equation}
%
The approximation holds for $R^f_t\approx1$ and small $\theta_t\Delta^b\tilde\Lambda^M_t$, which is appropriate given the calibration of $\theta_t$ to rare-event frequencies following \textcite{Barro2006}.  The promised yield $1/Q^b_t$ is used rather than the expected return $\mathbb{E}_t[R^b_{t+1}]$ because it is the object recorded in observed sovereign yield spreads, and therefore the object against which $\Delta^b$ is calibrated and against which the Spread-at-Risk statistics of Section~\ref{sec:results} are reported. The two differ by the expected loss: from~\eqref{eq:A3_ERb}, $\mathbb{E}_t[R^b_{t+1}] -R^f_t=R^f_t\,\theta_t\Delta^b(\tilde\Lambda^M_t-1)/H^b_t$, which strips out the actuarially fair component and retains only the risk premium.  At the baseline calibration the promised-yield spread is roughly $1.6$ times the expected-return spread; the distinction is therefore quantitatively material and is maintained throughout. 



Disaster risk propagates to real activity through two distinct channels, operating on different horizons and through different equations. The first is immediate and requires no default to ever occur, while the second requires a realised loss and therefore only activates with a lag.

\paragraph{Immediate channel: perceived risk via the bank's incentive constraint.}
 
A rise in $\theta_t$ tightens the sovereign bond's resilience $H^b_t$ (equation~\eqref{eq:A3_resil}) and, through it, the current bond price $Q^b_t$, before any default is realised. Because $R^b_{t+1}=1/Q^b_t$, this immediately revises the bank's expectation of next period's bond return, and the marginal values of net worth and of assets in the banker's value function $\nu_t,\eta_t$ are themselves conditional expectations over exactly this object. The leverage multiplier $\lambda_t=\nu_t/(\theta^b-\eta_t)$ therefore moves in the same period as the shock, tightening or loosening permitted balance-sheet size before bank net worth $N^b_t$ has changed at all, A $N^b_t$ is predetermined by~\eqref{eq:A3_Bank_Value} and cannot respond within the period. This channel is thus active for any $\theta_t$ innovation, realised or not, and its sign depends on how the shock moves the bank's portfolio margin $(1\phi)R^S_t+\phi\mathbb{E}_t[R^b_{t+1}]-R^d_t$ relative to the risk-free rate, not mechanically on the direction of $\theta_t$ alone.


\paragraph{Lagged channel: realised default via bank net worth.}
 
The second channel requires an actual default and transmits with a one-period lag, through the mark-to-market loss on bonds already held. The complete propagation from a realized sovereign default to real activity, with each arrow citing the equation responsible:
 
\begin{align*}
  &x_t=1 \;\text{(realised sovereign default)}
  \;\xrightarrow{\eqref{eq:A3_Rb}}\;
    R^b_t = \frac{1-\Delta^b}{Q^b_{t-1}}\downarrow
  \;\xrightarrow{\eqref{eq:A3_Bank_Value}}\;
    N^b_t\downarrow \;\text{(bank net worth)} \\[15pt]
  &\;\xrightarrow{\eqref{eq:A323}\,\&\,\eqref{eq:A3_homebias}}\;
    Q_tS_t \downarrow \;\text{(loan supply)}
  \;\xrightarrow{\text{\eqref{eq:loanmarketclearing}}}\;
    \bigl(Q_t K_{t+1} - N^e_t\bigr)\downarrow,\quad
    R^S_t\uparrow \;\text{(bank margin)} \\[15pt]
  &\;\xrightarrow{\eqref{eq:A3_NE}}\;
    N^e_t \downarrow
  \;\xrightarrow{\eqref{eq:A3_EFP}}\;
    \bigl(\mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}] - R^S_t\bigr)\uparrow \;\text{(entrepreneur margin)}
  \;\Rightarrow\;
    I_t,\,Y_t,\,C_t \downarrow \;\text{recession}
\end{align*}

The two channels are not competing descriptions of the same mechanism but genuinely separate margins, distinguished by what they require and how quickly they act. The immediate channel operates through the bank's forward-looking portfolio choice and requires only a change in $\theta_t$; it is present even along a sample path on which $x_t=1$ never realizes, and its magnitude is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The lagged channel operates through realized losses on bonds already on the balance sheet and requires $x_t=1$to be activated. I construct this term silent for structural reasons, as the realised returns $R^b_t$ and $R^K_t$ entering the model's recursions are both coded at their disaster-free ($x=0$) branch by construction, so only the disaster probability $\theta_t$, not an actual default, propagates through the system under perturbation methods. For simulation of a disaster path, the realised return terms would reqcuire a rederivation with a realised disaster term and a solution method that admits discrete draws, as proposed for the long-term bond extension (Appendix~\ref{sec:LongTermBond}).

\subsection{Public Authority}\label{secmain:public_authority}


Following \textcite{Isore2017}, the public authority issues one-period sovereign bonds, collects lump-sum taxes from households, and sets the nominal interest rate via a Taylor-type rule. No government expenditure on goods enters the model and fiscal policy is reduced to the financing of outstanding debt through taxes and new bond issuance.
 
The government issues one-period nominal bonds $B_{t+1}$ at the beginning of period $t+1$. Outstanding bonds $B_t$ carry nominal gross interest rate $(1 + r_{t-1})$. In the disaster state $x_t = 1$, a fraction $\Delta^b$ of the face value is not repaid (partial sovereign default); in the BAU state ($x_t = 0$) full repayment is made. The flow budget constraint of the government is:
 
\begin{equation}
  T_t = R^b_t Q^b_{t-1} B^b_{t-1} - Q^b_t B^b_t 
  \tag{A.4.1}
  \label{eq:A4_govBC}
\end{equation}
%
where $p_t$ is the aggregate price level and $T_t$ are real lump-sum taxes. The budget is balanced every period, as tax revenues plus new bond issuance exactly cover the cost of outstanding debt service net of any disaster haircut. This eliminates public savings as a state variable and implies that taxes are entirely endogenous, adjusting to satisfy~\eqref{eq:A4_govBC} each period.
 
We are then left with determining the Fisher Equation and Taylor Rule. The nominal gross interest rate $r_t$ set by the Taylor rule below is linked to the real risk-free rate $R^f_t$, which is determined by the household deposit Euler in \ref{sec:households} via the Fisher equation
 
\begin{equation}
  R^f_t = \frac{1 + r_t}{\mathbb{E}_t[1 + \pi_{t+1}]}
  \tag{A.4.2}
  \label{eq:A4_fisher}
\end{equation}
%
where $\pi_{t+1} \equiv p_{t+1}/p_t - 1$ is the net inflation rate. This is a standard arbitrage condition between nominal and real returns. Further, the public authority sets the nominal interest rate $r_t$ on sovereign bonds according to a Taylor-type rule that targets the deviations of inflation from its steady-state value $\bar{\pi}$ and of detrended output from its steady-state level $\bar{Y}/\bar{z}$:
 
\begin{equation}
  r_t = \rho_r\, r_{t-1}
        + (1 - \rho_r)\!\left[
            \phi_\pi(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})
            + \phi_Y\!\left(\frac{Y_t}{z_t} - \bar{Y}\right)
            + \bar{r}
          \right]
  \tag{A.4.3}
  \label{eq:A4_taylor}
\end{equation}
% 
where $\rho_r \in (0,1)$ is the interest rate smoothing parameter, $\phi_\pi > 1$ the inflation response coefficient (Taylor principle), $\phi_Y > 0$ the output gap coefficient, and $\bar{r}$ the steady-state nominal rate. The disaster probability $\theta_t$ does not enter the Taylor rule directly; it affects $r_t$ only through its general equilibrium effects on $\pi_t$ and $Y_t$.

Whether consumers actually have a sound understanding and incorporate both principles has been studied by \textcite{Draeger2016}, which find that about 52\% build expectations which are in line with the Fisher equation, while 46\% include the Taylor rule relation in forming their expectations.
% \parencite[Eq.~11, Appendix~A.3, Eq.~A.21]{Isore2017} 
 
% \noindent\textit{Steady-state note:} At the non-stochastic steady state, $r_{t-1} = r_t = \bar{r}$ and $\pi_t = \bar{\pi}$, $Y_t/z_t = \bar{Y}$. The rule is trivially satisfied. $\bar{r}$ is pinned by the Fisher equation at steady state: $1 + \bar{r} = \bar{R}^f \cdot (1 + \bar{\pi})$.
 
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} The Taylor rule is stated in terms of the nominal rate $r_t$ and inflation $\pi_t$ (both stationary) and detrended output $Y_t/z_t$ (stationary by construction). No further detrending required. $\to$ Appendix~B.
 

\subsection{Market Clearing}\label{secmain:marketclosing}

Market clearing conditions impose the requirement that all markets clear simultaneously in equilibrium. Together with the optimality conditions from A.1--A.4, that is the case if the goods and labour market is cleared. Subsequently, we will derive the good market and reinstate the labour, capital services, sovereign bond and loan market clearing conditions.  
% 
\paragraph{Good Market} Every financial claim in this model (loans, deposits, sovereign bonds, entrepreneur and bank net worth) is held by one agent and owed by another. This means that trading or accumulating these claims reallocates resources already counted once and neither creates nor destroys them. The aggregate resource constraint follows from going back only the flows that connect the financial layer to real production and consumption. By the firm's income identity, the right-hand side equals aggregate output:
\begin{equation}
Y_t=C_t+I_t \tag{A.5.1}
\end{equation}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Labour and Capital Services Market}
\label{par:A512}
 
Aggregate demand for labour and capital services from intermediate goods firms equals aggregate labour supply from households. Integrating the cost-minimisation condition across intermediate firms $j \in [0,1]$:
 
\begin{align}
  \int_0^1 L_{j,t}\,dj = L_t 
  \tag{A.5.2}
    \label{eq:A5_labour} \\[4mm]
  \int_0^1 \tilde{K}_{j,t}\,dj = \tilde{K}_t 
  \tag{A.5.3}
\end{align}
%
These conditions are already used in the cost minimisation process during the derivation of the aggregate production function \eqref{eq:cost_minimisation_int} where it eliminates the firm-level labour demand from the aggregate resource constraint. At the equilibrium level, they ensure that the real wage $W^{\text{real}}_t$ and the real rental rate $P^{k,\text{real}}_t$ adjust to clear the labour and capital services market. 
 
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} Hours $L_{j,t}$ are stationary (no trend); condition holds unchanged in the detrended system. $\to$ Appendix~B.

% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} $\tilde{K}_{j,t}$ inherits the trend of $K_t$; divide through by $Z_t$. $\to$ Appendix~B.
 
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Sovereign Bond Market}
\label{par:A513}
Sovereign bond market clearing follows directly from the home-bias identity~\eqref{eq:A3_homebias}, combined with the institutional fact that banks are the sole domestic holders of government debt, $B_t^b \equiv B_t$. 

% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} Divide through by $z_t$. Both $Q^b_t B^b_t$ and $Q_tS_t$ grow at trend $z_t$; the ratio $\phi/(1-\phi)$ is dimensionless. Detrended form is identical. $\to$ Appendix~B.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Loan Market}
\label{par:loan_clearing}
The quantity $Q_tS_t$ appears on both sides of the credit market. From the entrepreneur's balance sheet~\eqref{eq:A3_entrep_BS}, $Q_tS_t = Q_tK_{t+1}-N^e_t$ is loan \emph{demand}, determined by the capital entrepreneurs wish to finance net of their own net worth. From the aggregated binding leverage constraint~\eqref{eq:A323} together with the home-bias identity~\eqref{eq:A3_homebias}, $Q_tS_t = (1-\phi)\lambda_t N^b_t$ is loan \emph{supply}, determined by bank net worth and the leverage limit. Their equality,
%
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:loanmarketclearing}
  Q_tK_{t+1}-N^e_t \;=\; (1-\phi)\,\lambda_t\,N^b_t, \tag{A.5.4}
\end{equation*}
%
is the loan market clearing condition. It is not an independent structural equation but the requirement that entrepreneur credit demand and bank credit supply coincide; equivalently, it pins the equilibrium loan rate $R^S_t$ at which the entrepreneur participation condition~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff} holds. As with deposit and sovereign bond market clearing, it is a consequence of the agents' optimality conditions rather than an addition to them.

\subsection{Calibration}

In this paper, we decide to calibrate targeted moments following \textcite{Isore2017} and fit parameters related to the banking sector to financial market data. Except for the entrepreneur and banking block (Appendix~\ref{sec:banking_block}), all parameters are calibrated identically to \textcite{Isore2017}. Several of their targets are themselves drawn from the broader macro-finance literature. Therefore, Table \ref{tab:calibration_one} shows the calibration values with their sourcing from \textcite{Isore2017}. In detail, they draw their baseline values for capital and productivity destruction from \textcite{Barro2008}, while persistence of log-disaster risk comes from \textcite{Gourio2012}. The standard derivation of disaster-risk innovation has been derived by themselves to match empirical evidence. Further, utility parameters have relied solely on \textcite{Gourio2012} as the valuation of leisure goes along with the Epstein-Zin preferences utility function form. For also following his degree of risk aversion, they argue that the literature finds values with statistical significance between 2-4\%; nonetheless, the degree size would not matter for the response outcome's sign, being in line with theorectical results. In regards to the discount factor $\beta_0$, I follow the exact specification ($\beta_0 = 0.9985$) by \textcite{Bernanke1999} and \textcite{Gertler2011}. However, choosing a discount value with \textcite{Isore2017} $\beta_0 = 0.99$ as in \textcite{Isore2017}, we can confirm their argument for our model set-up too, that this parameter plays no severe role for the hereby obtained results.

For the elasticity of intertemporal substitution, we follow \textcite{Isore2017} in setting the EIS  to 0.5 rather than the value of 2 used by \textcite{Gourio2012}. The two calibrations belong to different traditions as far as we can identify. First, there are models targeting asset-pricing moments to match the level, volatility, and cyclicality of the equity premium, which is often exactly 2 as in \textcite{Barro2008, Gourio2012}, but requires generally an EIS above unity. Second, the broader macro-consumption literature instead finds values below unity, with \textcite{Havranek2015}'s meta-analysis of 2{,}735 estimates across 169 studies reporting a mean close to 0.5. Since this paper targets macroeconomic and credit-market outcomes rather than asset-pricing moments the rationale for the higher value does not apply, and we adopt $\mathrm{EIS}=0.5$ accordingly, exactly as \textcite{Isore2017} do for the same reason. \textcite{Isore2017} themselves show the two calibrations produce qualitatively identical, sign-mirrored dynamics, so the choice is not first-order for the model's qualitative predictions, but it is the natural default given this paper's focus. For further standard Keynesian or Taylor Rule parameters, I replace the standardvliterature values by \textcite{Isore2017} with parameters as calibrated in the European Central Banks NAWM II calibrated model after \textcite{Coenen2018}

For the banking, entrepreneur and sovereign block that we newly integrate, two targets need discussion beyond Table~\ref{tab:calibration_frictions}'s Source column. The sovereign haircut $\Delta^b=0.37$ matches \textcite{Cruces2013}'s documented average haircut across 180 sovereign debt restructurings between 1970 and 2010. The home-bias share $\phi = 0.10$ is this paper's own calibration choice, set to be order-of-magnitude consistent with the domestic sovereign-bond concentration documented for euro-area banks by \textcite{Altavilla2017}. The remaining entrepreneur and banking parameters follow \textcite{Bernanke1999}, \textcite{Gertler2011}, and \textcite{Rannenberg2016}; as already noted in Table~\ref{tab:calibration_frictions}, these remain generic US/BGG-literature targets pending replacement with euro-area-specific moments.




Table \ref{tab:calibration_two} lists the non-policy parameters from \textcite{Bernanke1999} and \textcite{Gertler2011} against the decision for our model.

        \subsubsection{Limitations and Future Outlook}

While the model has been proven to show the firing of the disaster risk anticipation chanel, we where not yet able to implement such a version that the disaster actually realises. Further contributions should look into the developments of New Keynesian Models with financial frictions that incorporate Epstein-Zin preferences with disaster risk and a scenario in which the disaster actually materializes. 

Additionally, the existing model could be extended through implementing a long-term bond structure. We modelled a basic component of such along the development of this paper, however, this bond structure could not have been succesfully included until the submission of this work for the following reasons: first, the equation incorporates a disaster term $x_{t+1}$ explicitly, following

\begin{equation}
R^b_{t+1} = \frac{(1-x_{t+1}\Deltaᵇ)(1+\rho_\beta Q^b_{t+1})}{Q^b_t}   
\end{equation}

Under standard perturbation, the disaster term still has to be zero everywhere in any AR(1)-probability-shock GIRF, for the identical reason as the one-period bond, to actually make the lagged channel work. In addition to this long-term bond, one needs a deterministic MIT-shock experiment which forces the default at a chosen date. This would require a second, different solution method layered on top of the extension itself. Next to solving for $x_t$ exogenously, the long-duration bond's new state variable must be rederived for every equation touching $\frac{Q_bB}{Q_b}$, including Appendix C's closed-form steady state, which is currently derived for the one-period bond specifically.

Thus, we only present the component to be included in our future work in Appendix \ref{sec:LongTermBond}.

\subsection{Households}\label{sec:households}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------
% ======================================================================
%  APPENDIX A  ——  Household Optimality Conditions
%  (full Lagrangian + labeled FOCs)
% ======================================================================
Households have Epstein--Zin--Weil preferences
%
\begin{equation*}
  \tilde{V}_t = \Biggl[
    \bigl[C_t(1-L_t)^{\varpi}\bigr]^{1-\psi}
    +\,\beta_0
    \Bigl(\mathbb{E}_t\bigl[\tilde{V}_{t+1}^{1-\gamma}\bigr]\Bigr)^{\!
      \frac{1-\psi}{1-\gamma}}
  \Biggr]^{\!\frac{1}{1-\psi}}
\end{equation*}
%
and with introduction of $V = \tilde{V}_t^{1-\psi}$ and $\chi = 1- \frac{1-\gamma}{1-\psi}$, we rewrite to
%
\begin{equation*}
  V_t = 
    \bigl[C_t(1-L_t)^{\varpi}\bigr]^{1-\psi}
    +\,\beta_0
    \Bigl(\mathbb{E}_t\bigl[V_{t+1}^{1-\chi}\bigr]\Bigr)^{\!
      \frac{1}{1-\chi}}
\end{equation*}
%
where the original Epstein-Zin objective is \eqref{eq:EZW}.
%
In \textcite{Isore2017}, households hold sovereign bonds directly and the bond return is haircut by $e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^b)}$ in a disaster. Households there also choose utilisation and investment, holding physical capital directly. In this paper, households do not hold sovereign bonds directly as they deposit with banks, who hold bonds on their balance sheets (home bias $\phi$). They likewise do not hold physical capital directly as capital is purchased and held by entrepreneurs, financed by entrepreneur net worth and bank loans (Appendix~\ref{sec:banking_block}). Instead, households own a competitive capital-goods-producer sector and receive its profit $\Pi_t^{cp}$ as a dividend, alongside dividends $\Pi_t^f$ from monopolistically competitive firms.
%
\begin{equation*}
 C_t + T_t + D_t
\;=\;
W^{\mathrm{real}}_t\,L_t 
 + R^d_{t-1}\,D_{t-1} + \Pi_t^f+ \Pi_t^{cp}
\end{equation*}
%

\paragraph{Lagrangian.}
Define the certainty equivalent
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:CE}
  \mathcal{R}_t \;\equiv\;
  \Bigl(\mathbb{E}_t\bigl[V_{t+1}^{1-\chi}\bigr]\Bigr)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\chi}}
\end{equation*}
so that the Bellman equation for \eqref{eq:EZW} reads $V_t = [C_t(1-L_t)^{\varpi}]^{1-\psi}+\beta_0\,\mathcal{R}_t$. Attaching multiplier $\Lambda^B_t\geq 0$ to budget constraint \eqref{eq:budget}:
%

\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}_t \;=\;&\; V_t \\
&-\;\Lambda^B_t\!\left[C_t+T_t+D_t
  -W^{\mathrm{real}}_t L_t
  -R^d_{t-1}D_{t-1}-\Pi_t^f-\Pi_t^{cp}\right]
\end{align*}
%
Unlike \textcite{Isore2017}'s household, which additionally holds physical capital and chooses $\{I_t,u_t,K_{t+1}\}$ subject to a capital-accumulation constraint, the household's problem here contains no capital-related choice variable and no capital-accumulation constraint. Consequently there is no multiplier capital-accumulation multiplier $\Lambda^C_t$. The investment, utilisation, and capital-return margins that $\Lambda^C_t$ would otherwise generate are instead derived from the capital-goods producer's and entrepreneur's own optimisation problems in section \ref{sec:banking_block}.
%
\paragraph{First-Order Conditions.} The first-order conditions with respect to $C_t, L_t$ and $D_t$ are derived. Differentiating $V_t^{1-\psi}$ with respect to $C_t$ via the chain rule and setting equal to the budget multiplier, this condition pins the shadow value of one unit of income in value-function units.
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial C_t} = 0: &\quad
  \mathbb{E}_t\!\left(\frac{\Lambda^B_{t+1}}{\Lambda^B_t}\right)
  = \beta_0\,\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[
      \left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{\!-\psi}
      \!\!\left(\frac{1-L_{t+1}}{1-L_t}\right)^{\!\varpi(1-\psi)}
      \frac{V_{t+1}^{-\chi}}
           {\bigl(\mathbb{E}_t V_{t+1}^{1-\chi}\bigr)^{\frac{-\chi}{1-\chi}}}
    \right] \notag
\end{align*}
%
The partial derivative of $V_t$ with respect to $L_t$ satisfies
$\partial V_t/\partial L_t = -(\varpi C_t)/(1-L_t)\cdot\Lambda_t^B$;
combining with the labour-income term in the budget constraint yields
the standard intratemporal labour-leisure condition:
%
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial L_t} = 0:&\quad
  \frac{1-L_t}{C_t} = \frac{\varpi}{W^{\mathrm{real}}_t} \notag
\end{align*}
%
The Epstein-Zin stochastic discount factor (\textsc{sdf}), derived from the inter-temporal marginal rate of substitution over the recursive aggregator follows from the deposit first-order condition:
%
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial D_t} = 0: &\quad
  \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[Q_{t,t+1}\,R^d_t\right] = 1
\end{align*}
%
where $Q_{t,t+1}\equiv \Lambda^B_{t+1}/\Lambda^B_t$. Since none of the three multipliers above enters the (now absent) capital-accumulation constraint, all three conditions are unaffected in form by the removal of capital from the household's problem. 
%
We obtain the household's optimality conditions as

%
\begin{align}
     Q_{t,t+1} &= \beta_0
      \left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{\!-\psi}
      \!\!\left(\frac{1-L_{t+1}}{1-L_t}\right)^{\!\varpi(1-\psi)}
      \frac{V_{t+1}^{-\chi}}
           {\bigl(\mathbb{E}_t V_{t+1}^{1-\chi}\bigr)^{\frac{-\chi}{1-\chi}}} \tag{A.1.1}\label{eq:SDF} \\[6mm]
    C_t &= \frac{W^{real}_t(1-L_t)}{\varpi} \tag{A.1.2}\label{eq:HHO_C} \\[6mm]
  1 &= E_t[Q_{t,t+1}R_t^d] \tag{A.1.3}\label{eq:HHO_Deposit_Euler}
\end{align}
%
Equation~\eqref{eq:SDF} defines $Q_{t,t+1}$ state by state as a random variable measurable at $t+1$, not its conditional mean. The disaster
indicator $x_{t+1}$ enters $Q_{t,t+1}$ through the growth factor $\Gamma_{t+1}$, and different equilibrium conditions require different
moments of the resulting distribution: the deposit Euler~\eqref{eq:HHO_Deposit_Euler} and the risk-free rate~\eqref{eq:riskfree_rate} require $\mathbb{E}_t[Q_{t,t+1}]$, whereas any condition discounting a payoff that grows with the trend, like the Bellman recursion and the Calvo auxiliaries of Appendix~\ref{appsec:firms}, requires $\mathbb{E}_t[Q_{t,t+1}\Gamma_{t+1}]$.  Because $Q_{t,t+1}$ and $\Gamma_{t+1}$ are both functions of the same indicator $x_{t+1}$, these two moments are not related by $\mathbb{E}_t[Q\Gamma]=\mathbb{E}_t[Q]\,\mathbb{E}_t[\Gamma]$.  They are
evaluated separately in Appendix~\ref{sec:Stationarization}.

\paragraph{Capital-Goods Producer.}\label{par:CP}
A representative, perfectly competitive, household-owned capital-goods producer converts final-good investment into installed capital using the adjustment-cost technology $S(I_t/K_t)$ already introduced in Section~\ref{sec:households}, purchasing $I_t$ units of the final good at unit price and selling the resulting capital at the market price $Q_t$. Its static per-period profit, rebated lump-sum to the household as $\Pi^{cp}_t$, is
%
\begin{equation*}
  \Pi_t^{cp} = Q_t\left[I_t - \frac{\tau}{2}K_t\left(\frac{I_t}{K_t} - \frac{\bar{I}}{\bar{K}}\right)^2\right] - I_t
  \;=\; Q_t\,S\!\left(\frac{I_t}{K_t}\right)K_t - I_t
  \label{eq:CP_profit}
\end{equation*}
%
$K_t$ enters only as a scale variable (the aggregate installed capital stock, taken as given by the producer, and owned by entrepreneurs — see Appendix~\ref{sec:banking_block}). The producer is not preference-bearing and solves a static problem each period, so no Euler equation or new state variable is introduced. Maximising the capital good's producers profit over $I_t$ yields
%
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial {\Pi^{cp}}}{\partial I_t} = 0:\quad Q_t\left[1-\tau\left(\frac{I_t}{K_t}-\frac{\bar I}{\bar K}\right)\right]=1
\end{equation*}
%
which gives Tobin's $Q$:
%
\begin{equation}
  Q_t = \frac{1}{1-\tau\!\left(\dfrac{I_t}{K_t}-\dfrac{\bar{I}}{\bar{K}}\right)}
  \tag{A.1.4}\label{eq:CP_TobinsQ}
\end{equation}
%
identical in form to \textcite{Isore2017}'s household-derived Tobin's-Q relation, since the underlying adjustment-cost technology is unchanged. Only the agent solving the maximisation problem has moved from the household's joint utilisation-investment margin to the capital-goods producer's own problem to be consistent with the argument that household owned entrepreneurs make capital and investment decisions. At the steady state $I/K=\bar I/\bar K$ gives $\bar Q=1$ and $\overline{\Pi^{cp}}=0$, meaning that the producer earns zero profit along the balanced growth path, as a competitive technology with no rents should. Away from the steady state $\Pi_t^{cp}$ is generally small and second-order in the investment gap; it is tracked in the household budget constraint but drops out of the aggregate resource constraint, since $I_t$ is real final-good use regardless of how the transaction between producer and entrepreneur nets out privately.

\paragraph{Capital Pricing: Moving from Household Euler to Entrepreneur Indifference.}
\textcite{Isore2017} impose a capital Euler $\mathbb{E}_t[Q_{t,t+1}R^K_{t+1}]=1$ as a household optimality condition, pricing capital at the household's own risk-free discount rate; their $R^K_{t+1}$ combines the utilisation and investment-adjustment-cost margins into a single object, since their household chooses both jointly. In this paper, households hold no capital, so no such condition arises from the household's problem at all. Capital is instead priced by the entrepreneur's indifference condition $\mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}]=R^S_t$ (equation~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff}), which prices capital at the loan rate and added premium, reflecting the external finance premium entrepreneurs face. The technological definition of $R^K_{t+1}$ itself (equation~\eqref{eq:A3_RK}) decomposes cleanly into the utilisation margin (entrepreneur, Appendix~A.3.1) and the investment margin (capital-goods producer, equation~\eqref{eq:CP_TobinsQ} above) which were jointly determined by the household's combined FOC in \textcite{Isore2017}.
%
% The disaster does, however, propagate to the return on capital through the productivity channel.  The productivity process (Appendix~A.2, equation~\eqref{eq:prod_process_app}) retains the factor $e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\epsilon)}$ as a Stationarization device: in the detrended equilibrium system (Appendix~B), the rental rate $p^{k,\mathrm{real}}_{t+1}$ inherits a disaster loading because $P^{k,\mathrm{real}}_{t+1} \propto z_{t+1}^{1-\alpha}$ and $z_{t+1}$ is reduced by $(1-\epsilon)$ in a disaster.  Entrepreneurs therefore face a lower expected return on capital after a disaster even without physical destruction; the full derivation is in Appendix~A.2, equation~\eqref{eq:entr_ind}.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Firms}\label{appsec:firms}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------
The economy has final and intermediate good producers. The first faces a maximisation problem of final aggregated goods under monopolistic competition, while the latter first minimizes costs to maximize profits. 
 \subsubsection{Final Good Production}
 
\paragraph{Final Good Production Aggregation}
Following \textcite{Isore2017} in the origins of  \textcite{DixitStiglitz1977}, we assume that a representative final-goods firm aggregates intermediate varieties $Y_{j,t}$ into final output in monopolistic competition 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aggregator}
  Y_t = \left(\int_0^1 Y_{j,t}^{(\nu-1)/\nu}\,dj\right)^{\nu/(\nu-1)} \tag{A.2.1}
\end{equation}
%
The firm maximises its final aggregated goods given by 
%
\begin{equation*}
    \max_{Y_{j,t}} \quad p_t\left(\int_0^1Y_{j,t}^{(\frac{\nu-1}{\nu})}\right)^{\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}} - \int_0^1 p_{j,t}Y_{j,t} dj
\end{equation*}
%
Taking the first-order condition with respect to $Y_{j,t}$ and applying the chain rule:
%
\begin{equation*}
    p_t \cdot \frac{\nu}{\nu-1} \left(\int_0^1 Y_{j,t}^{\frac{\nu-1}{\nu}}\,dj\right)^{\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}-1} \cdot \frac{\nu-1}{\nu}\, Y_{j,t}^{\frac{\nu-1}{\nu}-1} = p_{j,t}
\end{equation*}
%
Simplifying the exponent of the integral, noting that $\frac{\nu}{\nu-1} - 1 = \frac{1}{\nu-1}$, and the exponent on $Y_{j,t}$, noting that $\frac{\nu-1}{\nu} - 1 = -\frac{1}{\nu}$:
%
\begin{equation*}
    p_t \left(\int_0^1 Y_{j,t}^{\frac{\nu-1}{\nu}}\,dj\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu-1}} Y_{j,t}^{-\frac{1}{\nu}} = p_{j,t}
\end{equation*}
%
Recognising that $\left(\int_0^1 Y_{j,t}^{\frac{\nu-1}{\nu}}\,dj\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu-1}} = \left(\int_0^1 Y_{j,t}^{\frac{\nu-1}{\nu}}\,dj\right)^{\frac{\nu}{\nu-1} \cdot \frac{1}{\nu}} = Y_t^{1/\nu}$:
%
\begin{equation*}
    p_t\, Y_t^{1/\nu}\, Y_{j,t}^{-1/\nu} = p_{j,t}
\end{equation*}
%
Solving for $Y_{j,t}$:
%
\begin{align*}
    \left(\frac{Y_{j,t}}{Y_t}\right)^{-1/\nu} &= \frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t} \\[3mm]
    \frac{Y_{j,t}}{Y_t} &= \left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu}
\end{align*}
%
Profit maximisation gives demand for variety $j$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:demand}
  Y_{j,t} = Y_t\left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu} \tag{A.2.2}
\end{equation}
%
Since we can constitute that the final good is the aggregation of prices times their intermediate quantities, we can write the aggregate price index as 

\begin{align*}
    p_t = \Big(\int_0^1 p_{j,t}^{1-\nu} dj\Big)^\frac{1}{1-\nu}
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Intermediate Good Production} \label{sec:int_good}
\paragraph{Cost Minimisation}
Before final goods can be aggregated, intermediate good producers use labor and capital as production inputs. To minimise production costs, they choose optimal factor quantities in each given time period. Therefore, we assume that these firms take the wage $W_t$, the rental rate of capital $P_t^k$ as well as nominal factor prices as given. The underlying production function requires these firms produce at least as much of the intermediate good as it is demanded at a given price. The intra-temporal intermediate firm problem results in 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost_minimisation_int}
    \min_{L_{j,t}, \tilde{K}_{j,t}} W_{t}L_{j,t} + P_t^k\tilde{K}_{j,t} \qquad \text{s.t.} \quad \tilde{K}^\alpha_{j,t}(z_tL_{j,t})^{1-\alpha} \geq \left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu}Y_t \tag{A.2.3}
\end{equation}
%
where $z_t$ denotes the level of productivity and $\tilde{K}_{j,t} \equiv u_tK_{j,t}$ expresses effective capital.
%
The Lagrangian is
%
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{L} = W_t L_{j,t} + P_t^k \tilde{K}_{j,t} - \Lambda_{j,t}^F\left[\tilde{K}_{j,t}^\alpha(z_t L_{j,t})^{1-\alpha} - \left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu}Y_t\right]
\end{equation*}
%
The first-order conditions with respect to $L_{j,t}$ and $\tilde{K}_{j,t}$ are:
%
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial L_{j,t}} = 0: \qquad & W_t = \Lambda_{j,t}^F(1-\alpha)\,\tilde{K}_{j,t}^\alpha\, z_t^{1-\alpha}\, L_{j,t}^{-\alpha} \\[6pt]
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \tilde{K}_{j,t}} = 0: \qquad & P_t^k = \Lambda_{j,t}^F\,\alpha\,\tilde{K}_{j,t}^{\alpha-1}(z_t L_{j,t})^{1-\alpha}
\end{align*}
%
Dividing the first condition by the second eliminates $\Lambda_{j,t}^F$:
%
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{W_t}{P_t^k} = \frac{(1-\alpha)\,\tilde{K}_{j,t}^\alpha\, z_t^{1-\alpha}\, L_{j,t}^{-\alpha}}{\alpha\,\tilde{K}_{j,t}^{\alpha-1}(z_t L_{j,t})^{1-\alpha}} = \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{\tilde{K}_{j,t}}{L_{j,t}}
\end{equation*}
%
which gives the optimal capital-labour ratio:
%
\begin{equation*}
    \tilde{K}_{j,t}^* = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{W_t}{P_t^k}\cdot L_{j,t}
\end{equation*}
%
Substituting into the binding constraint $\tilde{K}_{j,t}^\alpha(z_t L_{j,t})^{1-\alpha} = \left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu}Y_t$ yields factor demands. Since all intermediate firms are symmetric and face the same capital-labour ratio, we can aggregate across $j \in [0,1]$. Integrating the factor market clearing conditions gives aggregate labour $L_t = \int_0^1 L_{j,t}\,dj$ and aggregate effective capital $\tilde{K}_t = \int_0^1 \tilde{K}_{j,t}\,dj$, and evaluated at aggregated quantities gives $\tilde{K}_t^\alpha(z_t L_t)^{1-\alpha} = Y_t$. To derive the nominal marginal cost, substitute the optimal capital-labour ratio
$\tilde{K}_{j,t}^* = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{W_t}{P_t^k}\cdot L_{j,t}$
into the first-order condition for labour:
%
\begin{equation*}
    W_t = \Lambda_{j,t}(1-\alpha)
    \left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{W_t}{P_t^k}\right)^\alpha
    z_t^{1-\alpha}\,L_{j,t}^{\alpha-\alpha}
\end{equation*}
%
where $L_{j,t}^\alpha$ cancels. Solving for $\Lambda_{j,t}$:
%
\begin{equation*}
    \Lambda_{j,t} = \frac{W_t}{(1-\alpha)\,z_t^{1-\alpha}}
    \left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{P_t^k}{W_t}\right)^\alpha
    = \frac{1}{z_t^{1-\alpha}}
    \left(\frac{W_t}{1-\alpha}\right)^{1-\alpha}
    \left(\frac{P_t^k}{\alpha}\right)^\alpha
\end{equation*}
%
Since $z_t^{1-\alpha}$ in the denominator can be absorbed into the wage term, this gives the nominal marginal cost $\Lambda_t^F$ , which is independent of $j$ under constant returns to scale:
%
\begin{equation*}
    \Lambda_t^F = \left(\frac{P_t^k}{\alpha}\right)^\alpha
    \left(\frac{W_t/z_t}{1-\alpha}\right)^{1-\alpha}
\end{equation*}
%
Dividing by the aggregate price level $p_t$ gives real marginal cost:
%
\begin{equation*}
    mc_t \equiv \frac{\Lambda_t^F}{p_t} =
    \left(\frac{r_t^k}{\alpha}\right)^\alpha
    \left(\frac{w_t/z_t}{1-\alpha}\right)^{1-\alpha}
\end{equation*}
%
where $r_t^k \equiv P_t^k/p_t$ and $w_t \equiv W_t/p_t$ denote the real rental rate and real wage respectively. The aggregate real factor price conditions then follow from dividing the nominal first-order conditions by $p_t$ and invoking $\tilde{K}_t^\alpha(z_tL_t)^{1-\alpha} = Y_t$. Hence, this yields aggregate real term FOC's
%
\begin{equation*}
    w_t = mc^*_t(1-\alpha)z_t^{1-\alpha}\tilde{K}_t^\alpha L_t^{-\alpha} \tag{A.2.4}
\end{equation*}
%
and
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:resale_price}
    P_t^{k, real} = mc^*_t\cdot\alpha\cdot z^{1-\alpha}\tilde{K}_t^{\alpha-1} L_t^{1-\alpha}   \tag{A.2.5}
\end{equation*}
%
with
\begin{equation*}
    \tilde{K}_t \equiv u_t K_t \tag{A.2.6}
\end{equation*}
%
As we further follow \textcite{Gourio2012} for the disaster process, we assume that productivity grows at 

\begin{equation} \label{eq:prod_process}
    \frac{z_{t+1}}{z_t} = e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\epsilon_{z,t+1}} \tag{A.2.7}
\end{equation}
% 
\paragraph{Profit Maximisation}\label{appsec:profitmax}
We further assume Calvo price-setting, in which each intermediate good producer may reset its price with probability $1-\zeta$ in any given period, while with  probability $\zeta$ it keeps its price unchanged. A firm that resets its price  $p_{j,t}$ at time $t$ chooses it to maximise the present discounted value of  profits over all future periods in which the price may remain fixed, subject to the demand schedule derived in \eqref{eq:demand} holds in every future period 
$t+s$ in which $p_{j,t}$ remains fixed:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calvo_max}
    \max_{p_{j,t}}\; E_t \sum_{s=0}^\infty (\zeta)^s Q_{t, t+s}
    \left(p_{j,t}Y_{j,t+s} - W_{t+s}L_{j,t+s} - P^k_{t+s}\tilde{K}_{j,t+s}
    \right) \tag{A.2.8a}
\end{equation}
%
where $Q_{t, t+s} \equiv \Lambda_{t+s}^B/\Lambda_t^B$ is the stochastic discount factor inherited from the household problem, with $Q_t = 1$.
%
\begin{equation*}
    Y_{j,t+s} = \left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_{t+s}}\right)^{-\nu} Y_{t+s}
\end{equation*}
%
Nominal profits in period $t+s$ can be written as revenues minus total factor costs. Since total factor costs equal nominal marginal cost times output, i.e.\ $W_{t+s}L_{j,t+s} + P^k_{t+s}\tilde{K}_{j,t+s} = 
\Lambda_{t+s}^F Y_{j,t+s}$, substituting the demand schedule gives:
%
\begin{equation*}
    p_{j,t}Y_{j,t+s} - \Lambda_{t+s}^F Y_{j,t+s} 
    = \left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_{t+s}}\right)^{-\nu}Y_{t+s}
    \left(p_{j,t} - \Lambda_{t+s}^F\right)
\end{equation*}
%
Factoring $p_{t+s}^{\nu-1}$ from both terms and writing 
$mc_t^* \equiv \Lambda_t^F/p_t$ for the real marginal cost evaluated 
at the reset date, following \textcite{Isore2017}, the objective rewrites as:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calvo_intertemporal}
    \max_{p_{j,t}}\; E_t \sum_{s=0}^\infty (\zeta)^s Q_{t, t+s}\, p_{t+s}^{\nu-1} 
    Y_{t+s} \left(p_{j,t}^{1-\nu} - \Lambda_{t}^F\, p_{j,t}^{-\nu}\right) \tag{A.2.8b}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{First-order condition.}
Taking the derivative of \eqref{eq:calvo_intertemporal} with respect to 
$p_{j,t}$ and setting equal to zero:
%
\begin{equation*}
    E_t\sum_{s=0}^\infty (\zeta)^s Q_{t, t+s}\, p_{t+s}^{\nu-1} Y_{t+s}
    \left((1-\nu)\,p_{j,t}^{-\nu} + \nu\,\Lambda_{t}^F\, p_{j,t}^{-\nu-1}
    \right) = 0
\end{equation*}
%
Multiplying through by $p_{j,t}^{\nu+1}$ and noting $p_{j,t}$ does not depend 
on $s$:
%
\begin{equation*}
    E_t\sum_{s=0}^\infty (\zeta)^s Q_{t, t+s}\, p_{t+s}^{\nu-1} Y_{t+s}
    \left((1-\nu)\,p_{j,t} + \nu\,\Lambda_{t}^F\right) = 0
\end{equation*}
%
Solving for $p_{j,t}$:
%
\begin{equation*}
    p_{j,t}^* = \frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\,E_t
    \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^\infty (\zeta)^s Q_{t, t+s}\, 
    p_{t+s}^{\nu-1} Y_{t+s} mc_{t+s}^*}
    {\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^\infty (\zeta)^s Q_{t, t+s}\, 
    p_{t+s}^{\nu-1} Y_{t+s}}
\end{equation*}
%
Dividing numerator and denominator by $p_t^{\nu-1}$ and using 
$mc_{t+s}^* \equiv \Lambda_{t+s}^F/p_{t+s}$, this is equivalently expressed 
with $p_{t+s}^\nu$ in both. Since the optimal  reset price is identical across all resetting firms, $p_t^* = p_{j,t}^*$, and taking it as a ratio over the current price level gives:
%
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{p_t^*}{p_t} = \frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\,E_t
    \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^\infty (\zeta)^s Q_{t, t+s}
    \!\left(\frac{p_{t+s}}{p_t}\right)^{\!\nu} Y_{t+s}\,mc_{t+s}^*}
    {\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^\infty (\zeta)^s Q_{t, t+s}
    \!\left(\frac{p_{t+s}}{p_t}\right)^{\!\nu-1} Y_{t+s}}
\end{equation*}

Defining the gross reset inflation rate as $1+\pi_t^* \equiv p_t^*/p_{t-1}$  and the gross inflation rate as $1+\pi_t \equiv p_t/p_{t-1}$, such that $p_t^*/p_t = (1+\pi_t^*)/(1+\pi_t)$, the ratio above can be written recursively by introducing auxiliary variables $\Xi_{1_t}$ and $\Xi_{2_t}$:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reset_inflation}\tag{A.2.9}
    1 + \pi_t^* = \frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\frac{\Xi_{1_t}}{\Xi_{2_t}}(1+\pi_t)
\end{equation}
%
where the recursive auxiliary variables are defined as
%
\begin{align}
    \Xi_{1_t} &= Y_t mc_t^* + \zeta\, E_t\!\left[Q_{t,t+1}
    (1+\pi_{t+1})^\nu \,\Xi_{1_{t+1}}\right] \label{eq:aux1}\tag{A.2.10}\\[4pt]
    \Xi_{2_t} &= Y_t + \zeta\, E_t\!\left[Q_{t,t+1}
    (1+\pi_{t+1})^{\nu-1}\,\Xi_{2_{t+1}}\right] \label{eq:aux2}\tag{A.2.11}
\end{align}
%
with $mc_t^*$ the real marginal cost of production at time $t$.

\paragraph{Aggregated Inflation and Production}\label{appsec:agginflation} The aggregated price index of the economy consists of a share of firms $1-\zeta$ that are not able to reset their pricing levels of at given time $t$ and its remainder fraction $\zeta$ of the previous period price expressed as

\begin{equation*}
    p_t^{1-\nu}= (1-\zeta)p_t^{*1-\nu} + \zeta p_{t-1}^{1-\nu}  = \int_0^{1-\zeta} p_t^{*1-\nu}dj + \int_{1-\zeta}^1 p_{j,t-1}^{1-\nu}
\end{equation*}
%
so the inflation rate is expressed as 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:inflation_rate}
    (1+\pi_t)^{1-\nu} = (1-\zeta)(1+\pi_t^*)^{1-\nu} + \zeta \tag{A.2.12}
\end{equation}
%
This is helpful for determining the aggregate production function as we can make use of the budget constraint from the cost minimisation of intermediate firms in (\ref{eq:cost_minimisation_int}) and aggregating aross intermediate firms to get

\begin{equation*}
    \left(\frac{\tilde{K}}{L}\right)^\alpha z_{t}^{1-\alpha}\int_0^1 L_{j,t}dj = Y_t\int_0^1\left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu} dj
\end{equation*}
%
and since aggregated labour demand must equal labour supply ($\int_0^1 L_{j,t} dj = L$ the resource constraint from (\ref{eq:cost_minimisation_int}) can be further expressed as 

\begin{equation*}
    \tilde{K}_{j,t}^\alpha(z_tL_{j,t})^{1-\alpha} = Y_t\int_0^1\left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu} dj
\end{equation*}
%
so we result in the aggregate production function 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:agg_production}
Y_t = \frac{\tilde{K_t}^\alpha(z_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}}{\int_0^1(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t})^{-\nu}} = \frac{\tilde{K_t}^\alpha(z_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}}{\Omega} \tag{A.2.13}
\end{equation}
%
with $\Omega$ as price distortion measure due to relative dispersion in relative prices from sticky prices. With the Calvo-Pricing assumption, the inflation rate can be written in aggregated terms of $\Omega$:

\begin{align*}\label{eq:omega}
    \Omega =& \int_0^1\left(\frac{p_{j,t}}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu} dj = (1-\zeta)\left(\frac{p_t^*}{p_t}\right)^{-\nu} + p_t^\nu\int_{1-\zeta}^1p_{{j,t-1}}^{-\nu} dj = p_t^{\nu}\int_0^1 p_t^{-\nu} \\[3mm]
    =& \quad (1-\zeta)\left(\frac{1+\pi_t}{1+\pi^*_t}\right)^{\nu} + \zeta(1+\pi_t)^{\nu}\Omega_{t-1} \tag{A.2.14}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Banking Block}\label{sec:banking_block}

This section derives the novel contribution of the paper. It combines the \textcite{Gertler2011} banking framework with \textcite{Bernanke1999} entrepreneur net-worth dynamics and a home-bias sovereign bond channel.

\subsubsection{Entrepreneurs}\label{subsec:entrepreneurs}

\paragraph{Setup and Contract Structure.}
The economy consists of a continuum of identical, risk-neutral entrepreneurs of mass one. Each purchase $K_{t+1}$ units of installed capital at the end of period~$t$ from the capital goods producer (Appendix~\ref{sec:households}) at price $Q_t$ and rents capital to intermediate goods firms in period $t=1$ at the real rental rate $P^k_{t+1}$. capital that is retained at the same period is valued at the same price $Q_t$ that applies to newly purchased units. The framing of buying and selling capital used throughout this section is a standard accounting convention in this literature and not a literal transaction. A continuing entrepreneur need not physically dispose of capital it retains, since resale and purchase occur at the same price. Each entrepreneur has net worth $N^e_t$ and finances the gap $Q_tK_{t+1} - N^e_t$ by borrowing from a bank at gross real loan rate $R^S_t$, contracted at end of period~$t$.

An idiosyncratic productivity $\omega_{j,t+1}$ is drawn i.i.d.\ with $\mathbb{E}[\omega] = 1$ and c.d.f.\ $F(\cdot)$. The bank cannot observe $\omega_{j,t+1}$ without paying a monitoring cost $\mu \in (0,1)$ of the entrepreneur's gross revenue, which is the costly state verification (CSV) problem of \textcite{Townsend1979}. The optimal contract specifies a default threshold $\bar{\omega}_t$ such that entrepreneur $j$ defaults if and only if $\omega_{j,t+1} < \bar{\omega}_t$. Because entrepreneurs are risk-neutral, they absorb aggregate risk and the participation constraint holds state-by-state after aggregate shocks to $R^K_{t+1}$ realise \parencite[Section~III]{Bernanke1999}.We assume the reduced-form external finance premium as derived in \textcite[pp. 1352]{Bernanke1999} as given.  Consequently, $\bar\omega_t$ does not appear as a separate endogenous variable below.

$K_{t+1}$ here denotes the quantity committed to at the end of period $t$, before $x_{t+1}$ is reaslized while the capital law of motion's $K_{t+1}$ denotes the 
the same object after the disaster realization, related by the Gourio factor.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Utilisation Choice.}
\label{par:A311a}
 
Given capital $K_{t+1}$ acquired at $t$, the entrepreneur chooses period-$(t{+}1)$ utilisation $u_{t+1}$ to maximise gross revenue from the capital stock. Doing so takes as given the rental price $P^{k,real}_{t+1}$ as derived in the intermediate good prodcuers cost minimisation resulting in equation~\ref{eq:resale_price} and the resale price $Q_{t+1}$ set by the capital-goods producer (Equation~\ref{eq:CP_TobinsQ}). Since the disaster factor $e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)}$ and the capital stock $K_{t+1}$ scale total revenue multiplicatively and do not depend on $u_{t+1}$, maximising realised revenue (for any realisation of $x_{t+1}$ and any idiosyncratic draw $\omega_{j,t+1}$) is equivalent to maximising per-unit revenue:
%
\begin{equation*}
  \max_{u_{t+1}}\;\;   P_{t+1}^{k, real}\,u_{t+1} + Q_{t+1}\bigl(1-\delta_0 u_{t+1}^{\eta}\bigr)
\end{equation*}
%
This objective is well-defined independently of the debt contract's default threshold $\bar\omega_t$. Therefore, utilisation maximises the total surplus available for division between entrepreneur and bank, not the entrepreneur's own share of it, so the choice is unaffected by contract details. The first-order condition is derived as 
%
\begin{equation}
  P_{t+1}^{k, real}  = Q_{t+1}\,\delta_0\,\eta\,u_{t+1}^{\eta-1}
  \tag{A.3.1.1}\label{eq:A3_util}
\end{equation}
%
with second-order condition $-Q_{t+1}\delta_0\eta(\eta-1)u_{t+1}^{\eta-2}<0$, showing that the standard convexity assumption on the depreciation function is satisfied for $\eta>1$ which has been already imposed throughout the model. 

\paragraph{Consistency with the Capital-Goods Producer.}
The two margins that \textcite{Isore2017}'s household jointly optimises are now split across two agents, but both continue to price the same
market-clearing capital good. The capital-goods producer's investment condition~\eqref{eq:CP_TobinsQ}, initiated at date $t+1$, and the
entrepreneur's utilisation condition~\eqref{eq:A3_util} both determine the single date-$(t{+}1)$ price $Q_{t+1}$:
%
The capital-goods producer's investment condition~\eqref{eq:CP_TobinsQ}, instantiated at date $t+1$, and the entrepreneur's utilisation condition~\eqref{eq:A3_util} both determine the single date-$(t{+}1)$ price $Q_{t+1}$:
%
\begin{equation*}
  Q_{t+1}
  \;=\;\frac{1}{1-\tau\!\left(\dfrac{I_{t+1}}{K_{t+1}}-\dfrac{\bar{I}}{\bar{K}}\right)}
  \;=\;\frac{P^{k,\mathrm{real}}_{t+1}}{\delta_0\,\eta\,u_{t+1}^{\eta-1}}.
\end{equation*}
%
Eliminating $Q_{t+1}$ between the two recovers exactly \textcite{Isore2017}'s single combined household condition:
%
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{1-\tau\!\left(\dfrac{I_{t+1}}{K_{t+1}}-\dfrac{\bar{I}}{\bar{K}}\right)} = \frac{P^{k,\mathrm{real}}_{t+1}}{\delta_0\,\eta\; u_{t+1}^{\,\eta-1}}
\end{equation*}
%
confirming that the two-agent decomposition is algebraically equivalent to the original joint FOC.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Return on Capital}
\label{par:return_on_capital}
 
Evaluated at the optimal utilisation \eqref{eq:A3_util}, the gross real return to holding one unit of capital from $t$ to $t+1$ is
 
\begin{equation}
  R^K_{t+1}
  = e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)}\,
    \frac{P^{k, real}_{t+1}\, u_{t+1}
          + Q_{t+1}\bigl(1 - \delta_0\, u_{t+1}^{\eta}\bigr)}
         {Q_t} \tag{A.3.1.2}
  \label{eq:A3_RK}
\end{equation}
%
where $u_{t+1}$ solves \eqref{eq:A3_util} and $\delta_{t+1} = \delta_0\, u_{t+1}^{\eta}$ is the resulting utilisation-dependent depreciation rate.
 
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} $R^K_{t+1}$ is a ratio of detrended prices and is already scale-invariant. No further detrending required. $\to$ Appendix~B.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{External Finance Premium.}
\label{par:A313}

The CSV contracting problem yields a loan contract under which the expected return the entrepreneur must generate on capital exceeds the loan rate it is charged, by a wedge that is decreasing in the entrepreneur's equity share in total capital.  This wedge is the external finance premium; it is distinct from, and additional to, the bank's own margin $R^S_t-R^d_t$, which is generated by the leverage constraint of Appendix~\ref{sec:A3.2} rather than by the contracting
problem.  We use the reduced form of \textcite{Bernanke1999}:

% A.3.1.3 -- REVISED: f(.) is the CSV premium schedule (definition of the
%            function), NOT a statement that the bank spread depends on
%            entrepreneur leverage. The premium enters the participation
%            condition A.3.1.6.
\begin{equation}
  f\!\left(\frac{N^e_t}{Q_t K_{t+1}}\right)
  \;\equiv\; f_0
    \left(\frac{N^e_t}{Q_t K_{t+1}}\right)^{\!-\chi^e},
  \qquad \chi^e > 0,\; f_0 > 0
  \label{eq:A3_EFP} \tag{A.3.1.3}
\end{equation}
%
where $N^e_t / Q_t K_{t+1}$ is the entrepreneur's equity share in total capital, with $Q_t$ the capital producer's price. When $N^e_t$ falls, the premium widens, amplifying the initial shock through the financial accelerator. The parameter $f_0$ is back-solved from a target target
steady-state entrepreneur premium $\mathbb{E}[\bar R^K]/\bar R^S$ given a target equity ratio $\bar N^e/\bar Q\bar K^{n}$, following \textcite{Christiano2014}; the closed form is $f_0=\mathcal{F}\,\varkappa^{\chi^e}$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:D7}).  The bank margin $\bar R^S/\bar R^d$ is a separate calibration target, matched through the leverage multiplier $\lambda$ rather than through $f_0$.\footnote{The reduced form is a local approximation around the calibrated equity ratio and is not normalised so that $f(1)=1$; at the calibration used here $f_0<1$, so the schedule should not be extrapolated to equity ratios near unity.}

% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} $N^e_t / Q_t K_{t+1}$ is a ratio of real quantities both growing at trend rate $Z_t$; the ratio is stationary after detrending by $Z_t$. Full stationarized form in Appendix~B. $\to$ Appendix~B.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Entrepreneur Net Worth Accumulation.}
\label{par:A314}

Following further \textcite{Bernanke1999}, a fraction $\sigma^e \in (0,1)$ of entrepreneurs survive each period and carry their net worth forward. The exiting fraction $(1-\sigma^e)$ transfers net worth to households as a lump sum. A startup transfer $\iota^e$ from households to entering entrepreneurs prevents
the entrepreneurs net worth to tend towards a value of zero:

\begin{equation}
  N^e_t
  = \sigma^e \!\left[
      \underbrace{R^K_t \cdot Q_{t-1}K_t}_{\text{gross return on capital}}
      - R^S_{t-1} \cdot \underbrace{\bigl(Q_{t-1}K_t - N^e_{t-1}\bigr)}_{\text{interest on externally financed capital}}
    \right]
    + \iota^e \tag{A.3.1.4}
  \label{eq:A3_NE}
\end{equation}
% 
In the standard \textcite{Bernanke1999} model without a banking sector, the lender's opportunity cost is the risk-free rate, so equation~\eqref{eq:A3_EFP} places the premium between $\mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}]$ and $R^f_t$ directly.  Here the lender is a constrained bank whose required return on the loan book is $R^S_t>R^d_t$, determined by the binding leverage constraint~\eqref{eq:A323} together with loan-market clearing (A.5.4).  The premium of~\eqref{eq:A3_EFP} then applies on top of $R^S_t$ rather than on top of $R^f_t$, which is the sense in which the two frictions stack.

% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} $R^K_t \cdot Q_{t-1}K_t$ has dimension $Z_{t-1}$. Divide through by $Z_{t-1}$ and apply the Gourio-trick growth normalization. Full stationarized form: $\hat{n}^e_t = \sigma^e e^{-\mu}[\cdots] + \hat{\iota}^e$. $\to$ Appendix~B.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Entrepreneur Balance Sheet Identity}
\label{par:A315}

The value to entrepreneurs equals the funding gap:

\begin{equation}
  Q_tS_t \;\equiv\; Q_t K_{t+1} - N^e_t \tag{A.3.1.5}
  \label{eq:A3_entrep_BS}
\end{equation}
%
which constitutes an accounting identity, defining the quantity $Q_tS_t$ that enters the bank balance sheet equation~\eqref{eq:A3_bankBS} and the leverage constraint equation~\eqref{eq:A323}.

% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} Divide through by $Z_t$. All three terms detrend directly. $\to$ Appendix~B.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Capital Demand: Free-Entry Condition}
\label{par:capital_market_clearing}
 
Financing of $K_{t+1}$ is decided at the end of period $t$, taking $Q_t$ (capital-goods producer, Appendix~\ref{sec:households}), the loan rate $R^S_t$, and the premium schedule~\eqref{eq:A3_EFP} as given.  An individual entrepreneur is atomistic relative to the continuum and therefore treats both $R^S_t$ and the aggregate equity ratio entering $f(\cdot)$ parametrically: it does not perceive its own $K_{t+1}$ choice as moving either. Expected end-of-period profit is then
%
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}]\,Q_tK_{t+1} - R^S_t\bigl(Q_tK_{t+1}-N^e_t\bigr)
  \;=\;
  \bigl(\mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}]-R^S_t\bigr)Q_tK_{t+1} + R^S_tN^e_t,
\end{equation*}
%
which is linear in $K_{t+1}$. A determinate, finite, positive level of capital demand is therefore an equilibrium only if the coefficient on $K_{t+1}$ vanishes: at any strictly positive coefficient the atomistic entrepreneur prefers $K_{t+1}\to\infty$, viceversa at any strictly negative coefficient it prefers $K_{t+1}=0$. Following \textcite{Gertler2011}, free entry and competitive credit markets impose exactly that 

%
% A.3.1.6 -- the participation / free-entry condition, where f(.) enters:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[R^K_{t+1}\right]
  = f\!\left(\frac{N_t^e}{Q_tK_{t+1}}\right) R^S_t
  \label{eq:A3_indiff} \tag{A.3.1.6}
\end{equation}
\textcite{Isore2017} impose the household capital Euler $\mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}] = R^f_t$ directly. In this model, entrepreneurs finance capital with bank loans, so the relevant no-arbitrage condition is against the loan rate, with $\mathbb{E}_t[R^K_{t+1}] > R^S_t \geq R^d_t = R^f_t$. Equation~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff} replaces their capital Euler and is the equation that connects the firm sector \ref{appsec:firms} to the banking sector \ref{sec:A3.2}. 

The rate wedge is the external finance premium derived in equation~\eqref{eq:A3_EFP}. Equation~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff} is a condition that $R^S_t$ must satisfy; it does not by itself determine $R^S_t$.  The equilibrium loan rate is pinned by the balance sheet chain rather than by ~~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff} itself. Given $N^b_t$ from~\eqref{eq:A3_Bank_Value} the binding leverage constraint~\eqref{eq:A323} and the home-bias identity~\eqref{eq:A3_homebias} fix loan supply $Q_tS_t=(1-\phi)\lambda N^b_t$; the entrepreneur balance sheet~\eqref{eq:A3_entrep_BS} then fixes $Q_tK_{t+1}=Q_tS_t+N^e_t$ and hence the equity ratio entering $f(\cdot)$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff} returns the $R^S_t$ consistent with that quantity. Loan-market clearing~\eqref{eq:loanmarketclearing} is the statement that these two routes to $Q_tS_t$ agree; it is a consequence of the optimality conditions, not an addition to them. This is the channel through which a sovereign loss on bank balance sheets raises the cost of credit to entrepreneurs.

The mechanics of that channel on how bank leverage is determined and how it responds to sovereign risk, are developed next.
 
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} Both sides are gross rates --- scale-invariant. $\to$ Appendix~B.

\paragraph{Banker Value Function and Optimisation}\label{par:A322}
 
A bank $j$ carries net worth $n_{j,t}$ in the end of period $t$ and solves a recursive programme to maximise the expected discounted value of lifetime payoffs to the owning household. With probability $\sigma^b\in(0,1)$ the banker survives to period $t+1$ and continues intermediating; with probability $1-\sigma^b$ it exits and returns $n_{j,t+1}$ directly to the household. All discounting is at the household stochastic discount factor $\Lambda^B_{t,t+1}$, since bankers are a share of the households. The Bellman equation is
 
\begin{equation*}
    V^b_t(n_{j,t})
    \;=\;
    \max_{\{L_{j,t},\,B^b_{j,t},\,D_{j,t}\}}\;
    \mathrm{E}_t\!\left[
        \Lambda^B_{t,t+1}
        \Bigl\{
            (1-\sigma^b)\,n_{j,t+1}
            +\sigma^b\,V^b_{t+1}(n_{j,t+1})
        \Bigr\}
    \right]
\end{equation*}
 
\noindent subject to the bank balance sheet identity \eqref{eq:A3_bankBS} and the incentive-compatibility condition derived below. 

Therefore, let $A_{j,t} \equiv Q_t S_{j,t}+\,Q^b_t B^b_{j,t}$ denote effective bank assets as defined in \eqref{eq:A3_bankBS}. The balance sheet identity gives $D_{j,t}=A_{j,t}-n_{j,t}$, so the deposit liability can be eliminated. Computing returns on each asset class and factoring out the deposit cost:
 
\begin{align*}
n_{j,t+1}
&= R^S_t\,Q_t S_{j,t}
 + R^b_{t+1}\,\,Q^b_t B^b_{j,t}
 - R^d_t\,D_{j,t}\\[4pt]
&= \underbrace{(R^S_t-R^d_t)\,Q_t S_{j,t}}_{\text{excess return on loans}}
 + \underbrace{(R^b_{t+1}-R^d_t)\,\,Q^b_t B^b_{j,t}}_{\text{excess return on bonds}}
 + R^d_t\,n_{j,t}.
\end{align*}
 
\noindent Defining the effective-asset-weighted excess return
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{R}^{\,\mathrm{ex}}_{t+1}
    \;\equiv\;
    \frac{(R^S_t-R^d_t)Q_t S_{j,t}
         +(R^b_{t+1}-R^d_t)\,\,Q^b_t B^b_{j,t}}
         {A_{j,t}},
\end{equation*}
net worth evolves as
\begin{equation*}
    n_{j,t+1} \;=\; \bar{R}^{\,\mathrm{ex}}_{t+1}\,A_{j,t}
                  \;+\; R^d_t\,n_{j,t}.
\end{equation*}
%
Under competitive pricing, $R^S_t$, $R^b_{t+1}$, and $R^d_t$ are aggregate prices, so $\bar{R}^{\,\mathrm{ex}}_{t+1}$ is an aggregate quantity independent of bank~$j$'s portfolio scale. We now want to prove that the period-$(t+1)$ value function is linear in both net worth and effective assets,
%
\begin{equation*}
    V^b_{t+1}(n_{j,t+1})
    \;=\;
    \nu_{t+1}\,n_{j,t+1} + \eta_{t+1}\,A_{j,t+1},
\end{equation*}
with a marginal value of a unit coefficients of net worth $\nu_{t+1}$ and effective assets $\eta_{t+1}$. Substituting into the continuation payoff:
%
\begin{equation*}
    (1-\sigma^b)\,n_{j,t+1}+\sigma^b\,V^b_{t+1}(n_{j,t+1})
    \;=\;
    \bigl[(1-\sigma^b)+\sigma^b\,\nu_{t+1}\bigr]\,n_{j,t+1}
    \;+\;\sigma^b\,\eta_{t+1}\,A_{j,t+1}.
\end{equation*}
%
Since the leverage constraint~(\ref{eq:A323}) derived below must bind in every period, $A_{j,t+1}=\lambda\,n_{j,t+1}$, where $\lambda$ is the steady-state leverage multiplier derived below, substituting and collecting terms yields
%
\begin{equation*}
    (1-\sigma^b)\,n_{j,t+1}+\sigma^b\,V^b_{t+1}(n_{j,t+1})
    \;=\;
    \underbrace{%
        \bigl[(1-\sigma^b)+\sigma^b\,(\nu_{t+1}+\lambda\,\eta_{t+1})\bigr]
    }_{\displaystyle\Omega_{t+1}}
    \;n_{j,t+1}
\end{equation*}
%
where $\Omega_{t+1}\equiv(1-\sigma^b)+\sigma^b(\nu_{t+1}+\lambda\,\eta_{t+1})$
is the survival-weighted continuation value. The Bellman reduces to maximising $\mathrm{E}_t[\Lambda^B_{t,t+1}\,\Omega_{t+1}\,n_{j,t+1}]$ over $A_{j,t}$. Substituting the net worth law of motion and
separating the terms in $n_{j,t}$ and $A_{j,t}$:
 
\begin{align*}
V^b_t(n_{j,t})
&= \max_{A_{j,t}}\;
   \mathrm{E}_t\!\left[
       \Lambda^B_{t,t+1}\,\Omega_{t+1}
       \bigl(
           \bar{R}^{\,\mathrm{ex}}_{t+1}\,A_{j,t}+R^d_t\,n_{j,t}
       \bigr)
   \right]\\[6pt]
&= \underbrace{%
       \mathrm{E}_t\!\left[
           \Lambda^B_{t,t+1}\,\Omega_{t+1}\,R^d_t
       \right]
   }_{\displaystyle \nu_t}
   \!\cdot n_{j,t}
   \;+\;
   \underbrace{%
       \mathrm{E}_t\!\left[
           \Lambda^B_{t,t+1}\,\Omega_{t+1}\,
           \bar{R}^{\,\mathrm{ex}}_{t+1}
       \right]
   }_{\displaystyle \eta_t}
   \!\cdot A_{j,t}.
\end{align*}
 
\noindent Both $\nu_t$ and $\eta_t$ are aggregate quantities: they
depend only on aggregate prices and the stochastic discount factor,
not on bank~$j$'s portfolio scale. The linear hypothesis is verified.
Denoting the two coefficients explicitly,
\begin{align*}
\nu_t &\;=\;
    \mathrm{E}_t\!\left[
        \Lambda^B_{t,t+1}\,\Omega_{t+1}\,R^d_t
    \right],\\[4pt]
\eta_t &\;=\;
    \mathrm{E}_t\!\left[
        \Lambda^B_{t,t+1}\,\Omega_{t+1}\,
        \frac{(R^S_t-R^d_t)Q_tS_t
             +(R^b_{t+1}-R^d_t)\,\,Q^b_t B^b_t}
             {A_t}
    \right],
\end{align*}
the value function is
\begin{equation}\tag{A.3.2.2}\label{eq:Banker_Value}
    V^b_t(n_{j,t}) \;=\; \nu_t\,n_{j,t} \;+\; \eta_t\,A_{j,t}
\end{equation}
%
Linearity holds because the bank is a price-taker in both asset markets, meaning the excess returns $R^K_{t+1}-R^d_t$ and $R^b_{t+1}-R^d_t$ are determined by aggregate conditions and are independent of bank~$j$'s balance sheet size.
 
\paragraph{Incentive Constraint and Leverage Limit.}
 
The diversion condition requires that the continuation value of honest
operation exceeds the private gain from diverting the fraction
$\theta^b$ of managed assets:
\begin{equation*}
    V^b_t(n_{j,t})
    \;\geq\;
    \theta^b\,
    \bigl[Q_t S_{j,t}+\,Q^b_t B^b_{j,t}\bigr]
    \;=\;
    \theta^b\,A_{j,t}.
\end{equation*}
Substituting the linear form~(\ref{eq:Banker_Value}):
\begin{equation*}
    \nu_t\,n_{j,t}+\eta_t\,A_{j,t}\;\geq\;\theta^b\,A_{j,t}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    A_{j,t}
    \;\leq\;
    \frac{\nu_t}{\theta^b-\eta_t}\,n_{j,t}
    \;\equiv\;
    \lambda_t\,n_{j,t}.
\end{equation*}
Bankers expand assets to the limit whenever $\eta_t>0$ and therefore the
franchise value of asset holdings is positive relative to the outside option of
depositing at $R^d_t$, so the constraint binds with equality in equilibrium. This gives the individual bank's leverage constraint
 
\begin{equation}\tag{A.3.2.3}\label{eq:A323}
    \,Q_t S_{j,t}+Q^b_t B^b_{j,t}
    \;\leq\;
    \lambda_t\,N^b_{j,t}
\end{equation}
% 
and in aggregated terms, since it binds for every $j$ and is linear, integrating over $j\in[0,1]$ gives 
%
\begin{equation*}
    \,Q_t S_{t}+Q^b_t B^b_{t}
    \;\leq\;
    \lambda_t\,N^b_{t} \qquad \text{where} \qquad S_t = \int_0^1 S_{j,t}
\end{equation*}
 
\noindent with time-varying multiplier $\lambda_t=\nu_t/(\theta^b-\eta_t)$.
In the full \textcite{Gertler2011} model, $\lambda_t$ must be solved each period from the recursions for $\nu_t$ and $\eta_t$. The baseline implementation of this paper uses an endogenous $\lambda_t$. This is the convention established in \textcite{Gertler2011}, who verify that the constraint always binds in a local neighbourhood of the steady state under empirically reasonable parameter values, and subsequently adopted as the baseline in the literature on unconventional monetary policy with
financial intermediaries \parencite{Gertler2013, Carlstrom2017, Sims2021}. The steady-state derivation of $\lambda$ from $\bar{\nu}$ and $\bar{\eta}$ is presented in Appendix~C.


% ============================================================
\subsubsection{Disaster Transmission Mechanism}
\label{sec:Disaster_Transition}

This subsection derives how $\theta_t$ propagates through the banking block. It establishes the bank's sovereign bond pricing condition from first principles before applying the \textcite{Gabaix2012} linearity-generating process to obtain a closed-form bond price. This paper sets $\Delta^k>0$ following \textcite{Isore2017}, so capital (and productivity) are destroyed proportionally in the disaster state and $\beta(\theta_t)$ is time-varying per equation~\eqref{eq:betatheta}. Since portfolio composition is fixed by the structural parameter $\phi$ the bank's single optimisation marginal is total asset scale $A_{j,t}$ which is set at $\lambda N^b_{j,t}$ by the binding leverage constraint \eqref{eq:A323}. The sovereign bond price $Q^b_t$ therefore clears the bond market at the fixed composition ratio rather than being determined by a separate portfolio first-order condition. The structural condition that defines the sovereign-bank channel is $\phi>0$ ($\Delta^b>0$ always): banks hold domestic bonds and absorb disaster losses through their balance sheets. The counterfactual $\phi=0$ removes the sovereign-bank channel while preserving the \textcite{Isore2017} capital channel, allowing a clean decomposition of the two transmission mechanisms. All disaster transmission through bonds operates via equation~\eqref{eq:A3_bondprice}.



\subparagraph{Derivation of Closed Form $\Lambda^M$}

Under the Epstein-Zin preference given in~\eqref{eq:SDF}, one can consider two date $(t, t{+}1)$
realisations that only differ in the disaster indicator $x_{t+1}$. Through the \textcite{Gourio2012} mechanism, the disaster acts as a scalar that multiplies all trend-growing quantities by $(1-\Delta^k)$ and given that $\tilde V_t$ and is homogeneous of degree one, so $C^{d}_{t+1}=(1-\Delta^k)C^{f}_{t+1}$ and $\tilde V^{d}_{t+1}=(1-\Delta^k)\tilde V^{f}_{t+1}$, while $L^d_{t+1} = L_{t+1}^f$. The certainty equivalent $\mathcal{R}_t(\cdot)$ is measurable at $t$ and identical across the two time realisations, hence

\begin{align*}
\Lambda^M &= \left(\frac{C^d_{t+1}}{C^f_{t+1}}\right)^{-\psi}\left(\frac{\tilde{V}^d_{t+1}}{\tilde{V}^f_{t+1}}\right)^{\psi-\gamma} \\[4mm] &= (1-\Delta^k)^{-\psi}(1-\Delta^k)^{\psi-\gamma} \\[4mm] &= (1 - \Delta^k)^{-\gamma} \qquad \text{(raw loading)}\tag{A.3.3.2b}\label{eq:A3_LambdaM}
\end{align*}
%
The object derived above is the ratio of the disaster-state SDF to its no-disaster realisation, $\Lambda^M \equiv M^{d}_{t+1}/M^{f}_{t+1} = (1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma}$, and depends on the disaster size and risk aversion alone.  The normalisation required by~\eqref{eq:A3_resil} follows by taking the conditional mean of the same object.  Since $M_{t+1}=M^{f}_{t+1}$ with probability $1-\theta_t$ and $M_{t+1}=\Lambda^M M^{f}_{t+1}$ with probability $\theta_t$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[M_{t+1}\right]}{M^{f}_{t+1}}
  = (1-\theta_t)+\theta_t\Lambda^M
  = 1+\theta_t\bigl(\Lambda^M-1\bigr)
  \;\equiv\;\mathcal{E}(\theta_t),
  \qquad
  \tilde\Lambda^M_t=\frac{\Lambda^M}{\mathcal{E}(\theta_t)}
  \tag{A.3.3.2c}
  \label{eq:A3_LambdaMtilde}
\end{equation}
%
$\mathcal{E}(\theta_t)$ is the same moment that governs the risk-free rate in equation~\eqref{eq:B_EQ}; the two appearances are not a coincidence but the same normalisation of the stochastic discount factor, entering once through the denominator of $R^f_t$ and once through the resilience of the defaultable bond.  Unlike $\Lambda^M$, $\tilde\Lambda^M_t$ is mildly time-varying through $\theta_t$; it is decreasing in $\theta_t$, so that a rise in disaster probability raises the spread less than proportionally.

\subsection{Public Authority}
\label{sec:public}
 
Following \textcite{Isore2017}, the public authority issues one-period sovereign bonds, collects lump-sum taxes from households, and sets the nominal interest rate via a Taylor-type rule. No government expenditure on goods enters the model; fiscal policy is reduced to the financing of outstanding debt through taxes and new bond issuance.
 
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Government Budget Constraint}
\label{par:A411}
 
The government issues one-period nominal bonds $B_{t+1}$ at the beginning of period $t+1$. Outstanding bonds $B_t$ carry nominal gross interest rate $(1 + r_{t-1})$. In the disaster state $x_t = 1$, a fraction $\Delta^b$ of the face value is not repaid (partial sovereign default); in the BAU state ($x_t = 0$) full repayment is made. The flow budget constraint of the government is:
 
\begin{equation}
  T_t = R^b_t Q^b_{t-1} B^b_{t-1} - Q^b_t B^b_t 
  \tag{A.4.1}
  \label{eq:A4_govBC}
\end{equation}
%
where $p_t$ is the aggregate price level and $T_t$ are real lump-sum taxes and the haircut structure follows \textcite{Gabaix2012}.
 
The budget is balanced every period: tax revenues plus new bond issuance exactly cover the cost of outstanding debt service net of any disaster haircut. This eliminates public savings as a state variable and implies that taxes are entirely endogenous, adjusting to satisfy~\eqref{eq:A4_govBC} each period.
 
\textcite{Isore2017} write the government budget constraint without an explicit haircut term (their Appendix A.3 does not model partial default; the bond disaster term enters only through the household's bond Euler). In this paper, $e^{x_t \ln(1-\Delta^b)}$ appears implicitly in equation~\eqref{eq:A4_govBC} to record the fiscal cost of sovereign partial default to the government. With $\Delta^k > 0$, the capital stock is affected by disaster through the Gourio factor $(1-\Delta^k)^{x_t}$ in the accumulation equation and the government budget constraint records the loss on debt service through $e^{x_t\ln(1-\Delta^b)}$.
 
We are then left with determining the Fisher Equation and Taylor Rule. 

\paragraph{Fisher Equation}

The nominal gross interest rate $r_t$ set by the Taylor rule below is linked to the real risk-free rate $R^f_t$ (determined by the household deposit Euler in \ref{sec:households}) via the Fisher equation:
 
\begin{equation}
  R^f_t = \frac{1 + r_t}{\mathbb{E}_t[1 + \pi_{t+1}]}
  \tag{A.4.2}
  \label{eq:A4_fisher}
\end{equation}
%
where $\pi_{t+1} \equiv p_{t+1}/p_t - 1$ is the net inflation
rate. This is a standard arbitrage condition between nominal and
real returns. 
 
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} Both sides are gross rates --- scale-invariant. $\to$ Appendix~B.
 
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Taylor Rule.}
\label{par:A413}
 
The public authority sets the nominal interest rate $r_t$ on
sovereign bonds according to a Taylor-type rule that targets the
deviations of inflation from its steady-state value $\bar{\pi}$
and of detrended output from its steady-state level $\bar{Y}/\bar{z}$:
 
\begin{equation}
  r_t = \rho_r\, r_{t-1}
        + (1 - \rho_r)\!\left[
            \phi_\pi(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})
            + \phi_Y\!\left(\frac{Y_t}{z_t} - \bar{Y}\right)
            + \bar{r}
          \right]
  \tag{A.4.3}
  \label{eq:A4_taylor}
\end{equation}
% 
where $\rho_r \in (0,1)$ is the interest rate smoothing parameter, $\phi_\pi > 1$ the inflation response coefficient (Taylor principle), $\phi_Y > 0$ the output gap coefficient, and $\bar{r}$ the steady-state nominal rate. The disaster probability $\theta_t$ does not enter the Taylor rule directly; it affects $r_t$ only through its general equilibrium effects on $\pi_t$ and $Y_t$.
% \parencite[Eq.~11, Appendix~A.3, Eq.~A.21]{Isore2017} 
 
% \noindent\textit{Steady-state note:} At the non-stochastic steady state, $r_{t-1} = r_t = \bar{r}$ and $\pi_t = \bar{\pi}$, $Y_t/z_t = \bar{Y}$. The rule is trivially satisfied. $\bar{r}$ is pinned by the Fisher equation at steady state: $1 + \bar{r} = \bar{R}^f \cdot (1 + \bar{\pi})$.
 
% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} The Taylor rule is stated in terms of the nominal rate $r_t$ and inflation $\pi_t$ (both stationary) and detrended output $Y_t/z_t$ (stationary by construction). No further detrending required. $\to$ Appendix~B.

\subsection{Model Closing Conditions}
\label{sec:A5}

% they close the model. Four markets require explicit clearing conditions: the labour market, the goods market, the deposit (bank liability) market and the public authority.
 
Market clearing conditions impose the requirement that all markets clear simultaneously in equilibrium. Together with the optimality conditions from A.1--A.4, that is the case if the goods and labour market is cleared. Subsequently, we will derive the good market and reinstate the labour, capital services as well as sovereign bond market clearing conditions.  

\subsubsection{Good Market Clearing}
% 
Every financial claim in this model (loans, deposits, sovereign bonds, entrepreneur and bank net worth) is held by one agent and owed by another. This means that trading or accumulating these claims reallocates resources already counted once and neither creates nor destroys them. The aggregate resource constraint follows from going back only the flows that connect the financial layer to real production and consumption.
%
Firms pay out their revenue as wages, capital rental, and profit:
\begin{equation*}
  Y_t = W_t^{\text{real}} L_t + P_t^{k,\text{real}}\tilde{K}_t + \Pi_t^f.
\end{equation*}
Capital rental income accrues to entrepreneurs, who use it, together with new bank borrowing, to purchase capital from the capital-goods producer and repay existing loans (equations~\eqref{eq:A3_NE}--\eqref{eq:A3_entrep_BS}); every entrepreneur-side flow beyond the rental income itself is a financial claim matched by a counterparty and cancels in aggregate, once startup and exit transfers between households and entrepreneurs are treated as negligible following \textcite{Gertler2011}'s own convention. Banks are funded by deposits and net worth and hold loans and bonds as assets (equations~\eqref{eq:A3_bankBS}, \eqref{eq:A3_Bank_Value}); the sovereign disaster haircut $\Delta^b$ redistributes resources from bondholders to the government rather than destroying them, and cancels against the identical term in the government budget constraint (A.4.1). The capital-goods producer's revenue from entrepreneurs' purchases nets against entrepreneurs' cost of that same purchase, leaving only its real resource use $I_t$, rebated to households as $\Pi_t^{cp}$ (equation~\eqref{eq:CP_profit}).

Eliminating $T_t$ as in \textcite{Isore2017}, we substitute the government budget constraint~\eqref{eq:A4_govBC} in real terms, dividing by $p_t$ yields
%
\begin{align*}
    T_t = \frac{B_t(1+r_{t-1})e^{x_t\ln(1-\Delta^b)}-B_{t+1}}{p_t}
\end{align*}
%
Using the bank's own bond pricing from \ref{eq:A3_bondprice} and \ref{eq:A3_Rb} to express it in the same unit as the bank already uses with the government's real financing identity is $T_t = R_t^bQ_{t-1}^bB_{t-1}^b - Q_t^bB_t^b$
%
Substituting these statements into the household budget ~\eqref{eq:budget} and substituting the bank balance sheet \ref{eq:A3_bankBS} for $D_t = Q_tS_t + Q_t^bB_t^b - N_t^b$, the bond value term $Q_t^bB_t^b$ cancels completely so 
%
\begin{align*}
W_t^{real}L_t+R_{t-1}^dD_{t-1}+\Pi^f+\Pi_t^{cp} &= C_t +R_t^bQ_{t-1}^bB_{t-1}^b−Q_t^bB_t^b+D_t \\[4mm]
&= C_t +R_t^bQ_{t-1}^b B_{t-1}^b + Q_tS_t - N_t^b
\end{align*}
%
Further using the entrepreneur's balance sheet identity and net worth accumulation functions, we obtain a flow identity 

\begin{equation*}
\underbrace{R_t^K Q_{t-1}K_t}_{\text{return}} + \underbrace{Q_tS_t}_{\text{new loan}}
+ \iota^e
=
\underbrace{R_{t-1}^S Q_{t-1}S_{t-1}}_{\text{repay old loan}} + \underbrace{Q_tK_{t+1}}_{\text{buy new capital}}
+ \underbrace{(1-\sigma^e) \left[ R_t^KQ_{t-1}K_t - R_{t-1}^SQ_{t-1}S_{t-1} \right]}_{\text{exit transfer to households}}.
\end{equation*}

By the definition of the return on capital~\eqref{eq:A3_RK}, $R_t^KQ_{t-1}K_t = P_t^{k,\mathrm{real}}\tilde K_t
+ Q_t\left(1-\delta_0u_t^\eta\right)K_t$, which is the return on capital equal the rental income plus resale value and moreover by the capital-goods producer's profit identity from \ref{par:CP}, the net payment entrepreneurs make to the producer for trading old capital in for new equals investment expenditure plus capital-producer profits $I_t + \Pi_t^{cp}$. Hence the resale-and-repurchase pair collapses to a single real cost:
\begin{equation*}
P_t^{k,\mathrm{real}}\tilde K_t - I_t - \Pi_t^{cp} + Q_tS_t + \iota^e = R_{t-1}^SQ_{t-1}S_{t-1} + (1-\sigma^e) \left[ R_t^KQ_{t-1}K_t - R_{t-1}^SQ_{t-1}S_{t-1} \right].
\end{equation*}
%
Substituting it into the government and bank merging result from above, using the $R_t^KQ_{t-1}S_{t-1}$-type loan-income terms, which cancel against the bank's net-worth accumulation equation (A.3.2.5) in the same way the bond terms do. This yields
\begin{equation*}
C_t+I_t =
W_t^{\mathrm{real}}L_t + P_t^{k,\mathrm{real}}\tilde K_t + \Pi_t^f.
\end{equation*}
%
Finally, by the firm's income identity, the right-hand side equals aggregate output:
\begin{equation}
Y_t=C_t+I_t \tag{A.5.1}
\end{equation}

It should be mentioned that the entrepreneurs and bankers start-up transfers $\iota^e, \iota$ and respectively their exit transfers they are pared with are assumed as in \textcite{Gertler2011} to be relatively small to aggregate resources and are therefore not separately taken into account in the household block. 


% \noindent\textit{Stationarization flag:} Divide through by $z_t$: $y_t = c_t + i_t$. \parencite[Eq.~A.38]{Isore2017} $\to$ Appendix~B.

\section{Stationarization}\label{sec:Stationarization}

This section conducts the detrending and stationarization of the models equation system. This is necessary as productivity $z_t$ grows at a rate $e^\mu$ per quarter, and therefore a generic real quantity $X_t$, will be written as $\hat{X}_t \equiv X_t/z_t$ for its detrended counterpart. One exception gives the value function $V_t \equiv \tilde{V}_t^{1-\psi}$, which aggregates consumption raised to the $(1-\psi)$ power and therefore inherits $z_t^{1-\psi}$, not $z_t$. We write $v_t \equiv \tilde{V}_t/z_t$, so that $V_t = v_t^{1-\psi}z_t^{1-\psi}$. Further, \textcite{Isore2017} use the \textcite{Gourio2012} trick to eliminate the binary disaster event $x_{t+1}$ from the equilibrium system, replacing it with the smooth probability $\theta_t$. We therefore define the detrended variables 

\begin{equation*}
  \hat{C_t} = C_t/z_t,\quad
  \hat{K_t} = K_t/z_t,\quad
  \hat{I}_t = I_t/z_t,\quad
  \hat{Y}_t = Y_t/z_t,\quad
  \hat{V}_t = V_t/z_t^{1-\psi}
\end{equation*}
%
Rates and prices already expressed as ratios of trend quantities require no transformation, since the trend cancels automatically in a ratio of two objects growing at the same rate, thus $Q_t$, $Q_{t,t+1}$, $R^K_t, R^S_t, R^f_t, R^d_t$, $u_t$, $L_t$, $\pi_t, \pi_t^*, \Omega_t$, and $mc_t$ are stationary as written. 
Where in~\ref{appsec:firms} already lowercase symbols are used for a real quantities rather than a trend-detrended one ($w_t \equiv W_t/p_t$, $r_t^k \equiv P_t^k/p_t$) we retain hats for productivity-trend detrending specifically, to prevent from colliding conventions. The stationarization in Appendix \ref{sec:stat_household} and \ref{sec:stat_firms} are guided by \textcite{Isore2017}, while Appendix B.3-B5 needed to be derived manually.

Three conditional moments of the disaster indicator recur throughout and are named once here.  All are instances of~\eqref{eq:gourio_star}
at different exponents $\kappa$:
%
\begin{equation*}
  \mathcal{D}(\theta_t)\equiv(1-\theta_t)+\theta_t(1-\Delta^k)^{1-\gamma},
  \quad
  \mathcal{E}(\theta_t)\equiv(1-\theta_t)+\theta_t(1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma},
  \quad
  \mathcal{K}(\theta_t)\equiv 1-\theta_t\Delta^k .
\end{equation*}
%
where $\mathcal{D}$ generates the pseudo-discount factor $\beta(\theta_t)$, $\mathcal{E}$ is carried by the stochastic discount factor, and $\mathcal{K}$ by the physical capital stock.  Each reduces to unity at $\Delta^k=0$ or $\theta_t=0$.  They are distinct objects and are not interchangeable.
 
 % Thus, we will stationarise the (1) household's utility function, capital and capital accumulation, FOC's on consumption, labor, investment and utilization rate, (2) firms detrended  optimality conditions and production function, (3) the entrepreneurs and banks XXXXXXXXXXX as well as (4) the public authorities monetary policy rule and aggregate resource constraint.

\paragraph{\textcite{Gourio2012} Trick}
As applied in \textcite{Isore2017}, we make use of a theoretical device that makes it feasable to solve the model with perturbation methods, even if a realised diaster component is present. It eliminates the binary disaster indicator $x_{t+1}$ from every expectation in which it appears, replacing it with its conditional first moment. Since $x_{t+1} \in \{0,1\}$ is Bernoulli with $\Pr_t(x_{t+1}=1)=\theta_t$, for any constant exponent $\kappa$,
%
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[e^{\kappa x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)}\right] 
  &= \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[(1-\Delta^k)^{\kappa x_{t+1}}\right] \\[4mm]
  &= (1-\theta_t)\cdot(1-\Delta^k)^0 + \theta_t\cdot(1-\Delta^k)^\kappa \\[4mm]
  &= (1-\theta_t)+\theta_t(1-\Delta^k)^\kappa \tag{B.$\star$}
  \label{eq:gourio_star}
\end{align*}
%
a direct consequence of $x_{t+1}$ taking only the values $0$ and $1$. Equation~\eqref{eq:gourio_star} is invoked repeatedly below with different values of $\kappa$. The pseudo-discount factor $\beta(\theta_t)$ already established in equation~\eqref{eq:betatheta} is exactly~\eqref{eq:gourio_star} evaluated at $\kappa=1-\gamma$, raised to the power $(1-\psi)/(1-\gamma)$ and scaled by $\beta_0$, which confirms that the identity reproduces an already-verified result before it is used to derive new ones.

\subsection{Households}\label{sec:stat_household}

\paragraph{Capital Accumulation.}
Starting from the level-form law of motion,
%
\begin{equation*}
  K_{t+1} = e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)}\Bigl[\left(1-\delta_0u_t^\eta\right)K_t+S\left(\frac{I_t}{K_{t}}\right)K_t\Bigr],
\end{equation*}
%
divide both sides by $z_{t+1}$. Using  \eqref{eq:prod_process}, the disaster factor on the left is 

\begin{align*}
    \frac{e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)}}{z_{t+1}} = \frac{1}{(z_te^{\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t+1}})}
\end{align*} 
%
It cancels state by state, not merely in expectation, because capital destruction and productivity destruction share the identical realization $x_{t+1}$. This is the structural reason for assuming the same $\Delta^k$ drives both processes as it allows the indicator to vanish from a level identity without requiring an expectation at all. With  $k_t \equiv K_t/z_t$, $i_t \equiv I_t/z_t$, and noting that $I_t/K_t=i_t/k_t$ since both share the same trend,
%
\begin{equation}
  k_{t+1} = e^{-\mu-\varepsilon_{z,t+1}}\Bigl[(1-\delta_0u_t^\eta)k_t+S\left(\frac{i_t}{k_t}\right)k_t\Bigr]
  \tag{B.1}
  \label{eq:B_capital}
\end{equation}
%
The disaster indicator has left the equation entirely as no expectation operator and $\theta_t$ remain.

\paragraph{Utility function and Stochastic Discount Factor.}
Substituting $V_t=v_t^{1-\psi}z_t^{1-\psi}$ and $C_t=c_tz_t$ into the Bellman equation 
%
\begin{align*}
    V_t=[C_t(1-L_t)^\varpi]^{1-\psi}+\beta_0(\mathbb{E}_t[V_{t+1}^{1-\chi}])^{1/(1-\chi)}
\end{align*}
%
every term acquires a common factor $z_t^{1-\psi}$, using
%
\begin{equation*}
  z_{t+1}^{(1-\psi)(1-\chi)}=z_t^{(1-\psi)(1-\chi)}e^{(1-\psi)(1-\chi)[x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t+1}]},
\end{equation*}
%
pulled through the expectation since $z_t$ is known at $t$. Dividing by $z_t^{1-\psi}$ yields
%
\begin{align*}
  v_t^{1-\psi} =& \bigl[c_t(1-L_t)^\varpi\bigr]^{1-\psi}+\beta_0\mathcal{R}^v_t,
  \qquad \text{with} \\[4mm]
  \mathcal{R}^v_t \equiv & \left(\mathbb{E}_t\Bigl[v_{t+1}^{(1-\psi)(1-\chi)}e^{(1-\psi)(1-\chi)[x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t+1}]}\Bigr]\right)^{\!1/(1-\chi)}
  \tag{B.2} \\
  \label{eq:B_bellman}
\end{align*}
%
For the stochastic discount factor \eqref{eq:SDF}, each factor is detrended in turn. 
%
The consumption-growth term contributes $(c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\psi}$ times a trend factor $e^{-\psi[x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t+1}]}$. The value-function ratio, using the substitution from~\eqref{eq:B_bellman}, contributes 
%
\begin{align*}
    \frac{e^{-\chi(1-\psi)[x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t+1}]}\cdot v_{t+1}^{-\chi(1-\psi)}}{(\mathcal{R}^v_t)^{-\chi}}
\end{align*}
%
The two trend exponents add to $\psi+\chi(1-\psi)$. Substituting $\chi \equiv 1-\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\psi}$ gives $\chi(1-\psi)=(1-\psi)-(1-\gamma)=\gamma-\psi$, so
%
\begin{equation*}
  \psi+\chi(1-\psi) = \psi+(\gamma-\psi) = \gamma.
\end{equation*}
%
Risk aversion alone survives in the cross-state scaling; the EIS parameter $\psi$ cancels here and appears only in the consumption-growth term itself.\footnote{This is the same cancellation underlying $\Lambda^M=(1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma}$ in equation~\eqref{eq:A3_LambdaM} --- the same mechanism recurring in a structurally distinct equation.} The detrended stochastic discount factor is therefore
%
\begin{equation}
  Q_{t,t+1} = \beta_0\left(\frac{c_{t+1}}{c_t}\right)^{\!-\psi}
  \!\!\left(\frac{1-L_{t+1}}{1-L_t}\right)^{\!\varpi(1-\psi)}
  \!e^{-\gamma[x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t+1}]}\,
  \frac{v_{t+1}^{-\chi(1-\psi)}}{(\mathcal{R}^v_t)^{-\chi}}
  \tag{B.3}
  \label{eq:B_SDF}
\end{equation}
%
The detrended value $v_{t+1}$ is invariant to the disaster draw.  This follows from the capital law of motion~\eqref{eq:B_capital}, in which
the indicator has already cancelled state by state: the detrended state vector entering period $t+1$ does not depend on $x_{t+1}$, and neither
therefore does $v_{t+1}$.  The scaling $\tilde V_{t+1}\big|_{x_{t+1}=1}=(1-\Delta^k)\tilde V_{t+1}\big|_{x_{t+1}=0}$ used in~\eqref{eq:betatheta} applies to the level value function $\tilde V_{t+1}=v_{t+1}z_{t+1}$, where the disaster enters through $z_{t+1}$ alone.  Consequently the entire disaster loading of the stochastic discount factor in~\eqref{eq:B_SDF} is carried by the explicit trend factor $e^{-\gamma x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)}$.
%
Writing $Q^{f}_{t,t+1}$ for the no-disaster realisation ($x_{t+1}=0$),
%
\begin{equation}
  Q_{t,t+1} = Q^{f}_{t,t+1}\,(1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma x_{t+1}},
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  \frac{Q_{t,t+1}\big|_{x_{t+1}=1}}{Q^{f}_{t,t+1}}=(1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma}
  \tag{B.3a}
  \label{eq:B_SDF_split}
\end{equation}
%
which reproduces the disaster SDF loading $\Lambda^M$ of equation~\eqref{eq:A3_LambdaM} from the stationarised system, having derived it in Section~\ref{par:asset_specific_resilience} by a different route.  The agreement of the two derivations is the internal consistency check on this block.
 
Applying~\eqref{eq:gourio_star} at $\kappa=-\gamma$ gives the moment required by the deposit Euler~\eqref{eq:HHO_Deposit_Euler} and the risk-free rate~\eqref{eq:riskfree_rate},
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[Q_{t,t+1}\right]
  = Q^{f}_{t,t+1}\,\mathcal{E}(\theta_t),
  \qquad
  R^f_t=\frac{1}{Q^{f}_{t,t+1}\,\mathcal{E}(\theta_t)}
  \tag{B.3b}
  \label{eq:B_EQ}
\end{equation}
%
while applying it at $\kappa=1-\gamma$,w hich is the exponent obtained by combining the SDF loading $-\gamma$ with the growth factor's $+1$,  gives the moment required wherever a trending payoff is discounted,
%
\begin{equation}
  \Theta_t\;\equiv\;\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[Q_{t,t+1}\Gamma_{t+1}\right]
  = Q^{f}_{t,t+1}\,e^{\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t+1}}\,\mathcal{D}(\theta_t)
  \;<\;\mathbb{E}_t[Q_{t,t+1}]\cdot\mathbb{E}_t[\Gamma_{t+1}]
  \tag{B.3c}
  \label{eq:B_Theta}
\end{equation}
%
the strict inequality holding for $\gamma>1$.  It is $\Theta_t$ that carries $\beta(\theta_t)$ in closed form: at the non-stochastic steady state
$\Theta=\beta(\bar\theta)e^{(1-\psi)\mu}$, as shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:D2}.  

% Substituting $\mathcal{D}$ for $\mathcal{E}$ in~\eqref{eq:B_EQ} --- equivalently, treating the SDF and the growth factor as independent --- suppresses roughly half of the precautionary reduction in the risk-free rate that is the defining result of the rare-disaster literature \parencite{Rietz1988,Barro2006,Gourio2012}.

\paragraph{Consumption FOC}
Equation~\eqref{eq:HHO_C}, $\varpi C_t=W_t^{real}(1-L_t)$, is a static, within-period condition with no expectation operator and no dependence on $x_{t+1}$. Both sides carry exactly one power of $z_t$, which cancels to
%
\begin{equation}
  c_t = \frac{w_t^{real}(1-L_t)}{\varpi}
  \tag{B.4}
  \label{eq:B_HHO_C}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Capital-Goods Producer's Tobin's Q.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:CP_TobinsQ} is a static first-order condition of a producer solving a period-by-period problem: no expectation, without any $x_{t+1}$ dependence. Since $I_t/K_t=i_t/k_t$,
%
\begin{equation}
  Q_t = \frac{1}{1-\tau(i_t/k_t-\bar{i}/\bar{k})}
  \tag{B.5}
  \label{eq:B_TobinsQ}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Deposit Euler and Risk-Free Rate.}
The deposit Euler equation~\eqref{eq:HHO_Deposit_Euler} and the risk-free rate ~\eqref{eq:riskfree_rate} contains only $R_t^d$ which is by construction dimensionless and $Q_{t,t+1}$, which has been detrended in~\eqref{eq:B_SDF}, so no further detrending applies.



\subsection{Firms}\label{sec:stat_firms}
\paragraph{Real Wage and Rental Rate}
\begin{align*}
    w_t^{real}z_t &= mc_t^*(1-\alpha)z_t^{1-\alpha}\tilde{K}_t^\alpha L_t^{-\alpha} \qquad \text{with} \qquad \tilde{K}_t=\tilde{k}_tz_t \\[4mm]
    &= mc_t^*(1-\alpha)\tilde{k}_t^\alpha L_t^{-\alpha}\cdot z_t \qquad \text{resulting in }\\[4mm]
    w_t^{real} &= mc_t^*(1-\alpha)\tilde{k}_t^\alpha L_t^{-\alpha} \tag{B.6}
\end{align*}
%
The rental-rate condition follows identically. Since the exponent on $z_t$ is $(1-\alpha)+(\alpha-1)=0$, the trend cancels completely and $P_t^{k,real}$ requires no detrending:
%
\begin{equation}
  P_t^{k,real} = mc_t^*\,\alpha\,\tilde{k}_t^{\alpha-1}L_t^{1-\alpha} \tag{B.7}
  \label{eq:B_rental}
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Effective Capital.}
Dividing the definition $\tilde{K}_t \equiv u_tK_t$ by $z_t$ yields
%
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{k}_t = u_tk_t \tag{B.8}
  \label{eq:B_Ktilde}
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Reset Inflation and Calvo Recursions}
In equation~\eqref{eq:reset_inflation}, both $\Xi_{1t}$ and $\Xi_{2t}$ trend at the same rate as $Y_t$, so their ratio is trend-free without detrending either individually first:
%
\begin{equation}
  1+\pi_t^* = \frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\frac{\xi_{1t}}{\xi_{2t}}(1+\pi_t)
  \tag{B.9}
  \label{eq:B_reset_pi}
\end{equation}
%
Writing 
%
\begin{align*}
    \Xi_{1t} =& \xi_{1t}z_t \\[4mm]
    Y_t =& y_tz_t \\[4mm]
    \Xi_{1,t+1} =& \xi_{1,t+1}z_{t+1}\\[4mm]
    =& \xi_{1,t+1}z_te^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t+1}}
\end{align*}
in equation~\eqref{eq:aux1}, every term carries exactly one factor of $z_t$, which cancels:
%
\begin{equation}
  \xi_{1t} = y_tmc_t^*+\zeta\,\mathbb{E}_t\Bigl[Q_{t,t+1}(1+\pi_{t+1})^\nu\xi_{1,t+1}\,e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t+1}}\Bigr]
  \tag{B.10}
  \label{eq:B_Xi1}
\end{equation}
%
The growth factor appearing explicitly here is not a duplicate of any factor inside $Q_{t,t+1}$. The stochastic discount factor is a ratio of marginal utilities and is scale-free by construction, whereas $\Xi_{1t}$ trends with output and must be deflated. The product $Q_{t,t+1}\Gamma_{t+1}$ under the expectation is exactly the object $\Theta_t$ of~\eqref{eq:B_Theta}, and equations~\eqref{eq:B_Xi1} and~\eqref{eq:B_Xi2} are therefore discounted at $\Theta_t$ rather than at $1/R^f_t$.
%
\begin{equation}
  \xi_{2t} = y_t+\zeta\,\mathbb{E}_t\Bigl[Q_{t,t+1}(1+\pi_{t+1})^{\nu-1}\xi_{2,t+1}\,e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)+\mu+\varepsilon_{z,t+1}}\Bigr]
  \tag{B.11}
  \label{eq:B_Xi2}
\end{equation}
%
Aggregate inflation and price dispersion (equations~\eqref{eq:inflation_rate} and~\eqref{eq:omega}) involve only inflation rates and a lagged dispersion measure, neither of which carries a productivity trend. They therefore enter the stationarised system unchanged:
%
\begin{equation}
  (1+\pi_t)^{1-\nu}=(1-\zeta)(1+\pi_t^*)^{1-\nu}+\zeta
  \tag{B.12}
  \label{eq:B_agg_pi}
\end{equation}
%
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_t=(1-\zeta)\left(\frac{1+\pi_t}{1+\pi^*_t}\right)^{\!\nu}+\zeta(1+\pi_t)^{\nu}\Omega_{t-1}
  \tag{B.13}
  \label{eq:B_dispersion}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Aggregate Production Function.}
Dividing equation~\eqref{eq:agg_production} by $z_t$:
%
\begin{align*}
Y_t =& \frac{\tilde{K}_t^\alpha(z_tL_t)^{1-\alpha}}{\Omega_t} \\[4mm]
  y_t =&\frac{\tilde{K}_t^\alpha z_t^{-\alpha}L_t^{1-\alpha}}{\Omega_t} = \frac{(\tilde{K}_t/z_t)^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha}}{\Omega_t}  \\[4mm]
  y_t =& \frac{\tilde{k}_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha}}{\Omega_t} \tag{B.14}
  \label{eq:B_production}
\end{align*}

\subsection{Banking Block}
% ============================================================
% Conventions: lowercase = detrended by z_t ; Gamma_t = z_t/z_{t-1}
% P^{k,real}, Q, Q^b, u, all gross rates, H^b, Lambda^M are stationary.
% k^n_{t+1} = K_{t+1}/z_t  : capital committed at t, detrended by the
%             date-t trend (distinct from k_{t+1} = K_{t+1}/z_{t+1}).

% ============================================================
\begin{align}
% ---- A.3.1 Entrepreneurs ----
% A.3.1.1  Utilisation FOC — stationary, unchanged
P^{k,real}_{t+1} &= Q_{t+1}\,\delta_0\,\eta\,u_{t+1}^{\eta-1}
\tag{B.15}\\[4pt]
%
% A.3.1.2  Return on capital — stationary; isolate disaster factor
R^K_{t+1} &= e^{x_{t+1}\ln(1-\Delta^k)}\,\tilde{R}^K_{t+1},
\qquad
\tilde{R}^K_{t+1} \equiv \frac{P^{k,real}_{t+1}u_{t+1}+Q_{t+1}(1-\delta_0 u_{t+1}^\eta)}{Q_t}
\tag{B.16}\\[4pt]
%
% A.3.1.6  Entrepreneur indifference / EFP — integrate out x_{t+1} via (B.star), kappa=1
\mathbb{E}_t\!\big[R^K_{t+1}\big]
       &= (1-\theta_t\Delta^k)\,\mathbb{E}_t\!\big[\tilde{R}^K_{t+1}\big]
        = f\!\left(\frac{n^e_t}{Q_t\,k^{n}_{t+1}}\right) R^S_t
\tag{B.17}\\[4pt]
%
% A.3.1.4  Entrepreneur net worth — all bracket terms dated t-1; factor Gamma_t^{-1}
n^e_t
&= \sigma^e\,\Gamma_t^{-1}\Big[\,R^K_t\,Q_{t-1}k^n_t
   - R^S_{t-1}\big(Q_{t-1}k^n_t - n^e_{t-1}\big)\Big]
   + \iota^{\,e}
\tag{B.18}\\[4pt]
%
% A.3.1.5  Entrepreneur balance sheet — Q_t K_{t+1}/z_t = Q_t k^n_{t+1}
Q_ts_t &= Q_t\,k^n_{t+1} - n^e_t
\tag{B.19}\\[10pt]
%
% ---- A.3.2 Banks ----
% A.3.2.1  Bank balance sheet
Q_ts_t + Q^b_t b^b_t &= n^b_t + d_t
\tag{B.20}\\[4pt]
%
% A.3.2.3  Leverage constraint
Q_ts_t + Q^b_t b^b_t &\le \lambda_t\,n^b_t
\tag{B.21}\\[4pt]
\end{align}

\paragraph{Endogenous Leverage Multiplier.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:A323} states the leverage constraint in terms of $\lambda_t$ without specifying how the multiplier itself is determined; this is filled in here rather than in Appendix~\ref{sec:A3.2}, since $\lambda_t=\nu^b_t/(\theta^b-\eta^b_t)$ requires the value-function coefficients $\nu^b_t$ and $\eta^b_t$ of equation~\eqref{eq:Banker_Value}, and both turn out to already be stationary in level form. Neither coefficient carries the productivity trend: $V^b_t(n_{j,t})=\nu_t n_{j,t}+\eta_t A_{j,t}$ expresses a value in the same units as net worth and effective assets, both of which grow at $Z_t$, so the ratios $\nu_t\equiv V^b_t/n_{j,t}$ and $\eta_t\equiv V^b_t/A_{j,t}$ are trend-free, exactly as $Q_t$, $R^S_t$ and $R^d_t$ require no detrending in equation~\eqref{eq:B_SDF} and its neighbours. The level-form recursions of equations~\eqref{eq:Banker_Value}--\eqref{eq:A323} therefore pass into the stationarized system unchanged in form, subject only to relabelling $Q_tS_t,\,Q^b_tB^b_t,\,N^b_t,\,D_t$ by their detrended counterparts $Q_ts_t,\,Q^b_tb^b_t,\,n^b_t,\,d_t$ already introduced in equation~\eqref{eq:A3_bankBS}.

A further simplification, not exploited in Appendix~\ref{sec:A3.2} because it was not yet needed there, is available now that the leverage constraint fixes total effective assets: since the home-bias identity~\eqref{eq:A3_homebias} pins the portfolio split at every date, $Q_ts_{j,t}=(1-\phi)A_{j,t}$ and $Q^b_tb^b_{j,t}=\phi A_{j,t}$, the effective-asset-weighted excess return of equation~\eqref{eq:Banker_Value} collapses from a two-asset average into a single $\phi$-weighted expression depending only on aggregate rates,
\begin{equation*}
  \bar R^{\,\mathrm{ex}}_{t+1} \;=\; (1-\phi)\bigl(R^S_t-R^d_t\bigr)+\phi\bigl(R^b_{t+1}-R^d_t\bigr) \;=\; (1-\phi)R^S_t+\phi R^b_{t+1}-R^d_t.
\end{equation*}

Discounting proceeds at $Q_{t,t+1}$ rather than at $\Theta_t$: $\nu^b_t$ and $\eta^b_t$ price a claim to a stationary payoff --- a share of net worth or of effective assets, both already detrended --- exactly as the deposit Euler~\eqref{eq:HHO_Deposit_Euler} discounts the stationary $R^d_t$ at $Q_{t,t+1}$ rather than at $\Theta_t$, the latter being reserved for payoffs that themselves grow with the trend (equation~\eqref{eq:B_Theta}).

With these two substitutions, the banker's value-function coefficients, the survival-weighted continuation value, and the leverage multiplier they jointly determine are
%
\begin{align}
  \eta^b_t &= \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[Q_{t,t+1}\,\Omega^b_{t+1}\,\bar R^{\,\mathrm{ex}}_{t+1}\right],
  \qquad
  \bar R^{\,\mathrm{ex}}_{t+1} \equiv (1-\phi)R^S_t+\phi R^b_{t+1}-R^d_t
  \tag{B.21a}\label{eq:B_etab}\\[6pt]
  %
  \nu^b_t &= \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[Q_{t,t+1}\,\Omega^b_{t+1}\,R^d_t\right]
  \tag{B.21b}\label{eq:B_nub}\\[6pt]
  %
  \Omega^b_t &= (1-\sigma^b) + \sigma^b\bigl(\nu^b_t+\lambda_t\,\eta^b_t\bigr)
  \tag{B.21c}\label{eq:B_Omegab}\\[6pt]
  %
  \lambda_t &= \frac{\nu^b_t}{\theta^b-\eta^b_t}
  \tag{B.21d}\label{eq:B_lambdat}
\end{align}
%
Equations~\eqref{eq:B_etab}--\eqref{eq:B_lambdat} form a self-contained recursive block, distinct in kind from the accumulation-type recursions elsewhere in this appendix: $\Omega^b_t$ is a static function of the \emph{current}-period $\nu^b_t,\eta^b_t,\lambda_t$, while $\eta^b_t$ and $\nu^b_t$ themselves discount the \emph{next}-period continuation value $\Omega^b_{t+1}$ --- the same object appearing one step ahead in its own defining equation. The multiplier $\lambda_t$ is consequently genuinely time-varying, matching the corrected derivation in Appendix~\ref{sec:A3.2}, rather than fixed at its steady-state value.

\begin{align}
% A.3.2.4  Home-bias identity (phi ratio unaffected by detrending)
Q^b_t b^b_t &= \frac{\phi}{1-\phi}\,Q_ts_t
\tag{B.22}\\[4pt]
%
% A.3.2.5  Bank net worth — all bracket terms dated t-1; factor Gamma_t^{-1}
n^b_t
&= \sigma^b\,\Gamma_t^{-1}\Big[(R^S_{t-1}-R^d_{t-1})\,Q_{t-1}s_{t-1}
   + (R^b_t-R^d_{t-1})\,Q^b_{t-1}b^b_{t-1}
   + R^d_{t-1}\,n^b_{t-1}\Big] + \iota
\tag{B.23}\\[10pt]
%
% ---- A.3.3 Disaster Transmission — all dimensionless, stationary ----
% A.3.3.2 / 2b  Resilience and SDF loading

\qquad        \tilde\Lambda^M_t = \frac{(1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma}}

The startup transfers $\iota^e$ and $\iota$ are constants in the detrended system, which requires the level-form transfers to scale with the productivity trend.

\section{Non-Stochastic Steady State}\label{sec:SteadyState}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Definition and Conventions}\label{sec:D0}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The steady state of the stationarised system of
Appendix~\ref{sec:Stationarization} is the fixed point at which every
detrended variable is constant and every innovation is set to zero.
Three conventions define it precisely and are used without further
comment throughout.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The disaster probability is at its mean, the disaster is not
      realised.}  We set $\theta_t=\bar\theta>0$ and $x_t=0$ for all $t$.
      This is the standard convention in the \textcite{Gourio2012}
      class of models: the indicator $x_{t+1}$ has been integrated out
      of every conditional expectation by~\eqref{eq:gourio_star}, so
      $\bar\theta$ survives inside expectations and prices risk, while
      the realised path is disaster-free.  Consequently $\bar\theta$
      enters the steady state \emph{only} through expectation
      operators; realised returns are evaluated at $x=0$.  Wherever
      the distinction matters --- the bond return \eqref{eq:A3_Rb} and
      the return on capital \eqref{eq:A3_RK} --- we write
      $\bar R^{b}$ and $\bar R^{K}$ for the realised ($x=0$) objects
      and $\mathbb{E}[\bar R^{b}]$, $\mathbb{E}[\bar R^{K}]$ for their
      conditional means.
\item \emph{The productivity innovation is off}, $\varepsilon_{z,t}=0$,
      as in the baseline of Section~2.1.  The
      gross growth factor is therefore
      $\bar\Gamma=e^{\mu}$.
\item \emph{Utilisation is normalised}, $\bar u=1$, so that
      $\bar\delta=\delta_0$ is the steady-state depreciation rate.  The
      utilisation curvature $\eta$ is then a calibration residual
      rather than a free parameter (Section~\ref{sec:D5}).
\end{enumerate}

\noindent It is convenient to record three moments of the disaster
indicator, all of them instances of~\eqref{eq:gourio_star} evaluated at
different exponents $\kappa$:
%
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{D}(\bar\theta)
    &\;\equiv\;(1-\bar\theta)+\bar\theta(1-\Delta^k)^{1-\gamma}
    &&(\kappa=1-\gamma)
    \tag{C.1a}\label{eq:D_Dcal}\\[2pt]
  \mathcal{E}(\bar\theta)
    &\;\equiv\;(1-\bar\theta)+\bar\theta(1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma}
     \;=\;1+\bar\theta\bigl(\Lambda^M-1\bigr)
    &&(\kappa=-\gamma)
    \tag{C.1b}\label{eq:D_Ecal}\\[2pt]
  \mathcal{K}(\bar\theta)
    &\;\equiv\;(1-\bar\theta)+\bar\theta(1-\Delta^k)
     \;=\;1-\bar\theta\Delta^k
    &&(\kappa=1)
    \tag{C.1c}\label{eq:D_Kcal}
\end{align}
%
with $\Lambda^M=(1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma}$ from~\eqref{eq:A3_LambdaM}. $\mathcal{D}$ is the moment that generates the pseudo-discount factor \eqref{eq:betatheta}; $\mathcal{E}$ is the moment carried by the stochastic discount factor itself and is also the normalisation  carried by the sovereign bond's resilience, $\tilde\Lambda^M=\Lambda^M/\mathcal{E}$ (equation~\eqref{eq:A3_LambdaMtilde}); it therefore enters the steady state twice, once through $\bar R^{f}$ and once through $\bar H^{b}$. $\mathcal{K}$ is the moment carried by the physical capital stock.  All three collapse to unity when $\Delta^k=0$ or $\bar\theta=0$, which is the formal sense in which the model nests \textcite{Isore2017} without capital destruction.  Keeping them distinct is essential: $\mathcal{D}$, $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{K}$ appear in different equations and substituting one for another mis-prices the risk-free rate.

\paragraph{Notation.}  Three symbols are used twice in
Appendix~\ref{AppendixA} and must be disambiguated before the bank
block can be solved.  Throughout this appendix the banker
value-function coefficients of~\eqref{eq:Banker_Value} are written
$\nu^b_t$ (marginal value of net worth), $\eta^b_t$ (marginal value of
assets) and $\Omega^b_{t+1}$ (survival-weighted continuation value), so
that $\nu$ remains the Dixit--Stiglitz elasticity of~\eqref{eq:aggregator},
$\eta$ the utilisation curvature of~\eqref{eq:A3_util}, and $\Omega_t$
the price-dispersion index of~(A.2.14).

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Preferences, Discounting and the Risk-Free Rate}\label{sec:D2}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Pseudo-discount factor.}
Evaluating~\eqref{eq:betatheta} at $\theta_t=\bar\theta$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \beta(\bar\theta)\;=\;\beta_0\,\mathcal{D}(\bar\theta)^{\frac{1-\psi}{1-\gamma}}
  \tag{C.2}\label{eq:D_betabar}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Stochastic discount factor.}
Write the detrended SDF~\eqref{eq:B_SDF} in the equivalent form
obtained by substituting $\chi(1-\psi)=\gamma-\psi$,
%
\begin{equation*}
  Q_{t,t+1}
  \;=\;\beta_0
  \left(\frac{c_{t+1}}{c_t}\right)^{\!-\psi}
  \left(\frac{1-L_{t+1}}{1-L_t}\right)^{\!\varpi(1-\psi)}
  \left(\frac{v_{t+1}\Gamma_{t+1}}
             {\bigl(\mathbb{E}_t\bigl[(v_{t+1}\Gamma_{t+1})^{1-\gamma}\bigr]\bigr)^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}}
  \right)^{\!\psi-\gamma}
  \Gamma_{t+1}^{-\psi}
\end{equation*}
%
in which the certainty equivalent has been written out explicitly.  At
the steady state $c_{t+1}=c_t$, $L_{t+1}=L_t$ and $v_{t+1}=\bar v$, so
the two growth-free ratios equal unity and, with $\varepsilon_z=0$,
$\Gamma_{t+1}=e^{\mu}(1-\Delta^k)^{x_{t+1}}$.  Then
$\bigl(\mathbb{E}_t[(\bar v\Gamma_{t+1})^{1-\gamma}]\bigr)^{1/(1-\gamma)}
 =\bar v\,e^{\mu}\mathcal{D}^{1/(1-\gamma)}$
by~\eqref{eq:gourio_star}, and the SDF in the disaster-free realisation
$x_{t+1}=0$ is
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar Q^{f}
  \;=\;\beta_0\,e^{-\psi\mu}\,\mathcal{D}^{\frac{\gamma-\psi}{1-\gamma}}
  \;=\;\frac{\beta(\bar\theta)\,e^{-\psi\mu}}{\mathcal{D}}
  \tag{C.3}\label{eq:D_Qf}
\end{equation}
%
while in the disaster realisation $x_{t+1}=1$ every occurrence of
$\Gamma_{t+1}$ contributes an extra factor $(1-\Delta^k)$, so that
$\bar Q^{d}/\bar Q^{f}=(1-\Delta^k)^{-\psi}(1-\Delta^k)^{\psi-\gamma}
 =(1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma}=\Lambda^M$.
This reproduces the closed form~\eqref{eq:A3_LambdaM} from the
stationarised SDF rather than from the level-form argument of
Section~\ref{par:asset_specific_resilience}, and is the internal
consistency check that the two derivations agree.

\paragraph{Risk-free and deposit rates.}
Taking expectations, $\mathbb{E}[\bar Q]=\bar Q^{f}\mathcal{E}$, so
\eqref{eq:riskfree_rate} gives
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar R^{f}
  \;=\;\frac{1}{\bar Q^{f}\,\mathcal{E}}
  \;=\;\frac{e^{\psi\mu}}{\beta_0\,
       \mathcal{D}^{\frac{\gamma-\psi}{1-\gamma}}\,\mathcal{E}}
  \;=\;\frac{\mathcal{D}\,e^{\psi\mu}}{\beta(\bar\theta)\,\mathcal{E}}\,,
  \qquad
  \bar R^{d}=\bar R^{f}
  \tag{C.4}\label{eq:D_Rf}
\end{equation}
%
where $\bar R^{d}=\bar R^{f}$ is deposit-market clearing
(Section~\ref{sec:households}).  At $\Delta^k=0$ or $\bar\theta=0$ we
have $\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{E}=1$ and~\eqref{eq:D_Rf} collapses to
$\bar R^{f}=e^{\psi\mu}/\beta_0$, the risk-free rate of the
disaster-free growth model.  The two moments enter with opposite signs:
$\mathcal{E}>1$ is the precautionary term that lowers the risk-free
rate as $\bar\theta$ rises, $\mathcal{D}>1$ (for $\gamma>1$) is the
pseudo-discounting term that partly offsets it.

\paragraph{Growth-adjusted discount factor.}
Several forward-looking equations (the Bellman
recursion~\eqref{eq:B_bellman} and the Calvo
auxiliaries~\eqref{eq:B_Xi1}--\eqref{eq:B_Xi2}) discount a variable that
grows with the trend, and therefore carry the product
$Q_{t,t+1}\Gamma_{t+1}$ rather than $Q_{t,t+1}$ alone.  Because the SDF
and the growth factor are both functions of the same indicator
$x_{t+1}$, the expectation of the product is \emph{not} the product of
the expectations.  Applying~\eqref{eq:gourio_star} at
$\kappa=(-\gamma)+1=1-\gamma$ to the combined exponent,
%
\begin{equation}
  \Theta\;\equiv\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bar Q\,\bar\Gamma\bigr]
  \;=\;\bar Q^{f}e^{\mu}\mathcal{D}
  \;=\;\beta(\bar\theta)\,e^{(1-\psi)\mu}
  \;<\;\mathbb{E}[\bar Q]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\bar\Gamma]
       \;=\;\bar Q^{f}e^{\mu}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{K}
  \tag{C.5}\label{eq:D_Theta}
\end{equation}
%
the inequality holding whenever $\gamma>1$.  The object that discounts
trending payoffs is thus exactly $\beta(\bar\theta)e^{(1-\psi)\mu}$,
and this --- not $\bar R^{f}$ --- is the natural place at which
$\beta(\bar\theta)$ appears in closed form.

\paragraph{Value function.}
Evaluating the Bellman recursion~\eqref{eq:B_bellman} at the steady
state, the certainty equivalent is
$\mathcal{R}^{v}=\bar v^{1-\psi}e^{(1-\psi)\mu}
 \mathcal{D}^{(1-\psi)/(1-\gamma)}$, so that
$\beta_0\mathcal{R}^{v}=\Theta\,\bar v^{1-\psi}$ and
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar v^{1-\psi}\bigl[1-\Theta\bigr]
  =\bigl[\bar c\,(1-\bar L)^{\varpi}\bigr]^{1-\psi}
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \bar v=\frac{\bar c\,(1-\bar L)^{\varpi}}
              {\bigl(1-\Theta\bigr)^{\frac{1}{1-\psi}}}
  \tag{C.6}\label{eq:D_v}
\end{equation}
%
which requires $\Theta<1$, i.e.\ $\beta(\bar\theta)e^{(1-\psi)\mu}<1$.
For $\psi>1$ this is weaker than $\beta_0<1$ and is satisfied for all
admissible calibrations; it is nonetheless the binding existence
condition for the household block and should be verified numerically.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Sovereign Bond Block}\label{sec:D3}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

From~\eqref{eq:A3_resil} and~\eqref{eq:A3_bondprice},
%
\begin{align}
 \bar H^{b} &= 1-\bar\theta\,\Delta^b\,\tilde\Lambda^M,
  \qquad
  \tilde\Lambda^M=\frac{(1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma}}{\mathcal{E}(\bar\theta)}
  \tag{C.7a}\label{eq:D_Hb}\\[2pt]
  \bar Q^{b} &= \frac{\bar H^{b}}{\bar R^{f}} .
  \tag{C.7b}\label{eq:D_Qb}
\end{align}
%
The realised gross bond return follows from~\eqref{eq:A3_Rb} at $x=0$,
and its conditional mean from~\eqref{eq:A3_ERb}:
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar R^{b}\;=\;\frac{1}{\bar Q^{b}}\;=\;\frac{\bar R^{f}}{\bar H^{b}}
  \;>\;\bar R^{f},
  \qquad
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[\bar R^{b}\bigr]
  =\frac{1-\bar\theta\Delta^b}{\bar Q^{b}}
  =\frac{\bigl(1-\bar\theta\Delta^b\bigr)\bar R^{f}}{\bar H^{b}} .
  \tag{C.8}\label{eq:D_Rb}
\end{equation}
%
The strict inequality is not an arbitrage violation: along the
disaster-free path banks collect the sovereign risk premium every
period without ever bearing the haircut, which is exactly what the
non-stochastic steady state of a rare-disaster model represents.  It
does mean that $\bar R^{b}$, not $\mathbb{E}[\bar R^{b}]$, is the object
that enters the bank net-worth accumulation~\eqref{eq:A3_Bank_Value} at
the steady state, since that equation is stated in realised returns.
The promised-yield spread~\eqref{eq:A3_spread} is
%
\begin{equation}
 \overline{\mathrm{spread}}
  =\frac{1}{\bar Q^{b}}-\bar R^{f}
  =\bar R^{f}\,\frac{\bar\theta\,\Delta^b\,\tilde\Lambda^M}{\bar H^{b}}
  \tag{C.9}\label{eq:D_spread}
\end{equation}
%
which is increasing in $\bar\theta$, in $\Delta^b$ and --- through
$\Lambda^M=(1-\Delta^k)^{-\gamma}$ --- in both risk aversion and the
size of the capital disaster.  The last dependence is the sense in
which the sovereign spread in this model is a genuine \emph{risk}
premium rather than a pure expected-loss compensation: with $\gamma=0$
the spread would collapse to $\bar R^{f}\bar\theta\Delta^b/\bar H^b$.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Price Setting}\label{sec:D4}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Steady-state inflation $\bar\pi$ is a policy parameter.  Inverting the
aggregate inflation condition~\eqref{eq:B_agg_pi},
%
\begin{equation}
  1+\bar\pi^{*}
  =\left[\frac{(1+\bar\pi)^{1-\nu}-\zeta}{1-\zeta}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\nu}} ,
  \tag{C.10}\label{eq:D_pistar}
\end{equation}
%
and solving the dispersion recursion~\eqref{eq:B_dispersion} for its
fixed point,
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar\Omega
  =\frac{(1-\zeta)\Bigl(\dfrac{1+\bar\pi}{1+\bar\pi^{*}}\Bigr)^{\nu}}
        {1-\zeta\,(1+\bar\pi)^{\nu}} ,
  \tag{C.11}\label{eq:D_Omega}
\end{equation}
%
which requires $\zeta(1+\bar\pi)^{\nu}<1$.  At $\bar\pi=0$ both reduce
to $\bar\pi^{*}=0$ and $\bar\Omega=1$.

The Calvo auxiliaries~\eqref{eq:B_Xi1}--\eqref{eq:B_Xi2} discount a
trending payoff and therefore carry $\Theta$ from~\eqref{eq:D_Theta}:
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar\xi_{1}=\frac{\bar y\,\overline{mc}}{1-\zeta\,\Theta\,(1+\bar\pi)^{\nu}},
  \qquad
  \bar\xi_{2}=\frac{\bar y}{1-\zeta\,\Theta\,(1+\bar\pi)^{\nu-1}} ,
  \tag{C.12}\label{eq:D_xi}
\end{equation}
%
both requiring $\zeta\Theta(1+\bar\pi)^{\nu}<1$.  Substituting
into the reset-price condition~\eqref{eq:B_reset_pi} and solving for
marginal cost,
%
\begin{equation}
  \overline{mc}
  =\frac{\nu-1}{\nu}\,
   \frac{1+\bar\pi^{*}}{1+\bar\pi}\;
   \frac{1-\zeta\,\Theta\,(1+\bar\pi)^{\nu}}
        {1-\zeta\,\Theta\,(1+\bar\pi)^{\nu-1}} .
  \tag{C.13}\label{eq:D_mc}
\end{equation}
%
At $\bar\pi=0$ this is the familiar $\overline{mc}=(\nu-1)/\nu$: the
inverse of the flexible-price mark-up.  Positive trend inflation raises
$\overline{mc}$ above that level.  Finally, the Fisher
equation~\eqref{eq:A4_fisher} pins the steady-state nominal rate,
%
\begin{equation}
  1+\bar r=\bar R^{f}\,(1+\bar\pi) ,
  \tag{C.14}\label{eq:D_r}
\end{equation}
%
at which the Taylor rule~\eqref{eq:A4_taylor} is satisfied identically.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Capital, Utilisation and the Return on Capital}\label{sec:D5}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

With $\bar i/\bar k=\bar I/\bar K$ the adjustment cost and its
derivative vanish, so Tobin's~$Q$~\eqref{eq:B_TobinsQ} gives
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar Q^{\mathrm{tob}}=1,
  \qquad
  \overline{\Pi^{cp}}=0 ,
  \tag{C.15}\label{eq:D_Qtob}
\end{equation}
%
confirming the zero-profit property of the capital-goods producer
asserted in Section~\ref{par:CP}.  The capital law of
motion~\eqref{eq:B_capital} at $\bar u=1$ and $S(\bar i/\bar k)\bar k=\bar i$
gives
%
\begin{equation}
  e^{\mu}\bar k=(1-\delta_0)\bar k+\bar i
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \bar i=\bigl(e^{\mu}-1+\delta_0\bigr)\bar k ,
  \tag{C.16}\label{eq:D_i}
\end{equation}
%
and effective capital is $\bar{\tilde k}=\bar u\bar k=\bar k$
by~\eqref{eq:B_Ktilde}.  The two dating conventions for the capital
stock are related by
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar k^{n}=e^{\mu}\,\bar k ,
  \tag{C.17}\label{eq:D_kn}
\end{equation}
%
since $k^{n}_{t+1}=K_{t+1}/z_t$ and $k_{t+1}=K_{t+1}/z_{t+1}$.  This
factor matters: $\bar k^{n}$ is the object that appears in the
entrepreneur balance sheet~\eqref{eq:A3_entrep_BS} (in detrended form, B.20) and in the equity
ratio of the external finance premium, whereas $\bar k$ is the object
that appears in the production function.

Evaluated at $\bar u=1$ and $\bar Q^{\mathrm{tob}}=1$, the disaster-free
component of the return on capital~\eqref{eq:A3_RK} (in detrended form, B.16) is
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\tilde R}^{K}=\bar P^{k,\mathrm{real}}+1-\delta_0 ,
  \qquad
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[\bar R^{K}\bigr]=\mathcal{K}(\bar\theta)\,\bar{\tilde R}^{K}
  =\bigl(1-\bar\theta\Delta^k\bigr)\bar{\tilde R}^{K} ,
  \tag{C.18}\label{eq:D_RK}
\end{equation}
%
with $\bar R^{K}=\bar{\tilde R}^{K}$ the realised return along the
disaster-free path.  Finally, the utilisation
condition~\eqref{eq:A3_util} at $\bar u=1$ reads
$\bar P^{k,\mathrm{real}}=\delta_0\eta$, which under the
normalisation of Section~\ref{sec:D0} pins the curvature parameter
%
\begin{equation}
  \eta=\frac{\bar P^{k,\mathrm{real}}}{\delta_0} .
  \tag{C.19}\label{eq:D_eta}
\end{equation}
%
The second-order condition $\eta>1$ therefore imposes the admissibility
restriction $\bar P^{k,\mathrm{real}}>\delta_0$ on the calibration: the
steady-state rental rate must exceed the depreciation rate, which is
the requirement that operating capital at full utilisation is
profitable at the margin.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Production and Aggregate Quantities}\label{sec:D6}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The factor-price conditions~(B.6)--\eqref{eq:B_rental}
depend on capital and labour only through the ratio
$\bar{\tilde k}/\bar L$.  Inverting the rental-rate condition,
%
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\bar{\tilde k}}{\bar L}
  =\left(\frac{\overline{mc}\,\alpha}{\bar P^{k,\mathrm{real}}}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},
  \qquad
  \bar w=\overline{mc}\,(1-\alpha)
        \left(\frac{\bar{\tilde k}}{\bar L}\right)^{\!\alpha}.
  \tag{C.20}\label{eq:D_kl}
\end{equation}
%
The production function~\eqref{eq:B_production} and the
investment relation~\eqref{eq:D_i} are both homogeneous of degree one
in $(\bar k,\bar L)$, so output, investment and --- by goods-market
clearing~\eqref{eq:stat_closing} --- consumption are all pinned per unit
of labour:
%
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\bar y}{\bar L}
  =\frac{1}{\bar\Omega}\left(\frac{\bar{\tilde k}}{\bar L}\right)^{\!\alpha},
  \qquad
  \frac{\bar i}{\bar L}
  =\bigl(e^{\mu}-1+\delta_0\bigr)\frac{\bar{\tilde k}}{\bar L},
  \qquad
  \frac{\bar c}{\bar L}=\frac{\bar y}{\bar L}-\frac{\bar i}{\bar L} .
  \tag{C.21}\label{eq:D_perL}
\end{equation}
%
The scale is then closed by the labour--leisure
condition~\eqref{eq:B_HHO_C}, $\varpi\bar c=\bar w(1-\bar L)$.  Dividing
by $\bar L$ and rearranging gives the closed form
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar L=\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{\varpi}{\bar w}\cdot\dfrac{\bar c}{\bar L}} ,
  \tag{C.22}\label{eq:D_L}
\end{equation}
%
after which $\bar k=(\bar{\tilde k}/\bar L)\bar L$,
$\bar y=(\bar y/\bar L)\bar L$, $\bar c$ and $\bar i$ follow.  Equation
\eqref{eq:D_L} treats the leisure weight $\varpi$ as a deep parameter
and lets hours be determined; the alternative convention --- targeting
$\bar L$ and inverting~\eqref{eq:D_L} for $\varpi$ --- is discussed in
the calibration section.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Entrepreneur Block}\label{sec:D7}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Let
%
\begin{equation}
  \varkappa\;\equiv\;\frac{\bar n^{e}}{\bar Q^{\mathrm{tob}}\bar k^{n}}
  \;=\;\frac{\bar n^{e}}{e^{\mu}\bar k}
  \tag{C.23}\label{eq:D_kappa}
\end{equation}
%
denote the steady-state entrepreneur equity ratio, the argument of the
external finance premium~\eqref{eq:A3_EFP}.  The free-entry
condition~\eqref{eq:A3_indiff} evaluated at the steady state is
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[\bar R^{K}\bigr]
  \;=\;f_0\,\varkappa^{-\chi^{e}}\,\bar R^{S}
  \;\equiv\;\mathcal{F}\,\bar R^{S},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{F}\equiv f_0\varkappa^{-\chi^{e}} ,
  \tag{C.24}\label{eq:D_partic}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathcal{F}>1$ is the gross external finance premium.  Combining
\eqref{eq:D_partic} with~\eqref{eq:D_RK} delivers the rental rate as a
function of the loan rate alone,
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar P^{k,\mathrm{real}}
  =\frac{\mathcal{F}\,\bar R^{S}}{1-\bar\theta\Delta^k}-(1-\delta_0) ,
  \tag{C.25}\label{eq:D_pkr}
\end{equation}
%
which is the equation that replaces \textcite{Isore2017}'s household
capital Euler $\mathbb{E}[Q_{t,t+1}R^{K}_{t+1}]=1$.  Two wedges separate
$\bar P^{k,\mathrm{real}}$ from its \textcite{Isore2017} counterpart:
the entrepreneur premium $\mathcal{F}$ and the bank margin implicit in
$\bar R^{S}>\bar R^{d}=\bar R^{f}$.  Setting
$\mathcal{F}=1$ and $\bar R^{S}=\bar R^{f}$ recovers their steady state
exactly.

The entrepreneur balance sheet~\eqref{eq:A3_entrep_BS} (in detrended form, B.20) gives loan demand
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar Q^{\mathrm{tob}}\bar s
  =\bar k^{n}-\bar n^{e}
  =(1-\varkappa)\,e^{\mu}\bar k ,
  \tag{C.26}\label{eq:D_QS}
\end{equation}
%
and net-worth accumulation~\eqref{eq:A3_NE} (in detrended form, B.19) --- in which every bracketed
term is dated $t-1$ and therefore carries $\bar\Gamma^{-1}=e^{-\mu}$ ---
becomes
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar n^{e}
  =\sigma^{e}\Bigl[\bigl(\bar{\tilde R}^{K}-\bar R^{S}\bigr)\bar k
    +\bar R^{S}e^{-\mu}\bar n^{e}\Bigr]+\iota^{e} ,
  \tag{C.27}\label{eq:D_ne}
\end{equation}
%
using $e^{-\mu}\bar k^{n}=\bar k$.  Solving,
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar n^{e}
  =\frac{\sigma^{e}\bigl(\bar{\tilde R}^{K}-\bar R^{S}\bigr)\bar k+\iota^{e}}
        {1-\sigma^{e}e^{-\mu}\bar R^{S}} ,
  \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
  \iota^{e}
  =\bar n^{e}\bigl(1-\sigma^{e}e^{-\mu}\bar R^{S}\bigr)
   -\sigma^{e}\bigl(\bar{\tilde R}^{K}-\bar R^{S}\bigr)\bar k .
  \tag{C.28}\label{eq:D_iotae}
\end{equation}
%
Existence of a positive, finite $\bar n^{e}$ requires
$\sigma^{e}e^{-\mu}\bar R^{S}<1$, i.e.\ the entrepreneur survival rate
must be low enough relative to the growth-adjusted loan rate for net
worth not to grow without bound.  In practice the second form is used:
$\varkappa$ is the calibration target and $\iota^{e}$ the residual
transfer that supports it.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Bank Block}\label{sec:D8}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Balance sheet.}
The home-bias identity~\eqref{eq:A3_homebias} together with the binding
leverage constraint~\eqref{eq:A323} gives the entire asset side as a
multiple of bank net worth,
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar A=\frac{\bar Q^{\mathrm{tob}}\bar s}{1-\phi}=\lambda\,\bar n^{b},
  \qquad
  \bar Q^{b}\bar b^{b}=\phi\lambda\bar n^{b},
  \qquad
  \bar d=(\lambda-1)\,\bar n^{b} ,
  \tag{C.29}\label{eq:D_bankBS}
\end{equation}
%
so that, combining with~\eqref{eq:D_QS}, loan-market
clearing~(A.5.4) determines bank net worth residually:
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar n^{b}=\frac{(1-\varkappa)\,e^{\mu}\bar k}{(1-\phi)\,\lambda} .
  \tag{C.30}\label{eq:D_nb}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Net-worth accumulation and the start-up transfer.}
Substituting the portfolio shares
$\bar Q^{\mathrm{tob}}\bar s=(1-\phi)\lambda\bar n^{b}$ and
$\bar Q^{b}\bar b^{b}=\phi\lambda\bar n^{b}$
into~\eqref{eq:A3_Bank_Value}, and using the \emph{realised} bond return
$\bar R^{b}=1/\bar Q^{b}$ of~\eqref{eq:D_Rb},
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar n^{b}
  =\sigma^{b}e^{-\mu}
    \Bigl\{\lambda\bigl[(1-\phi)(\bar R^{S}-\bar R^{f})
          +\phi(\bar R^{b}-\bar R^{f})\bigr]+\bar R^{f}\Bigr\}\bar n^{b}
   +\iota ,
  \tag{C.31}\label{eq:D_nbLOM}
\end{equation}
%
hence
%
\begin{equation}
  \iota=\bar n^{b}
  \Bigl(1-\sigma^{b}e^{-\mu}
    \bigl\{\lambda\bigl[(1-\phi)(\bar R^{S}-\bar R^{f})
          +\phi(\bar R^{b}-\bar R^{f})\bigr]+\bar R^{f}\bigr\}\Bigr) .
  \tag{C.32}\label{eq:D_iota}
\end{equation}
%
The bracketed object is the gross return on the bank's portfolio net of
funding costs, levered by $\lambda$; $\iota>0$ requires it to be smaller
than $e^{\mu}/\sigma^{b}$, the growth-adjusted survival-corrected upper
bound beyond which bank net worth would grow without limit and the
leverage constraint would eventually cease to bind.

\paragraph{Value-function coefficients and the diversion parameter.}
Appendix~\ref{par:A322} derives the recursions for $\nu_t,\eta_t,\Omega_t$ but defers the mapping to the underlying moral-hazard parameter $\theta^b$; that mapping is completed here. Write the asset-weighted excess return using the portfolio shares,
%
\begin{equation*}
  \bar R^{\,\mathrm{ex}}_{t+1} = (1-\phi)\bigl(R^S_t-R^d_t\bigr) + \phi\bigl(R^b_{t+1}-R^d_t\bigr)
\end{equation*}
%
Both $\nu_t$ and $\eta_t$ discount using the \emph{realised} (no-default, $x=0$) bond return $R^b_{t+1}=1/Q^b_t$, not the fully theta-weighted conditional expectation of the bond payoff. This is not a further approximation layered on top of the value-function derivation of Appendix~\ref{par:A322}: it is the same convention already used, without comment, by the net-worth accumulation equation~\eqref{eq:A3_Bank_Value} two paragraphs above, and by every other equation in this appendix in which $R^b_t$ appears. The two objects are not interchangeable in general --- $\mathbb{E}_t[R^b_{t+1}]=(1-\theta_t\Delta^b)/Q^b_t$ is strictly below the realised return $1/Q^b_t$, equation~\eqref{eq:A3_ERb} --- and the bond-pricing identity $\mathbb{E}_t[Q_{t,t+1}R^b_{t+1}]=1$, which would set the bond's contribution to $\eta_t$ to exactly zero, holds only for the former. Using it here would price the banker's continuation value against a hypothetical default branch that this appendix's own solution method never actually realises (Appendix~\ref{sec:banking_block}, the note on the absence of a realised $x_t=1$ regime): the value function must be consistent with the \emph{same} net-worth process it is the marginal value of, and that process only ever compounds the realised return. The bond's steady-state excess return over the risk-free rate is therefore not zero but exactly the steady-state sovereign spread, $\bar R^b-\bar R^f=\overline{\mathrm{spread}}$ (equation~\eqref{eq:A3_spread}), and it enters $\eta_t$ on the same footing as the loan spread. Defining
%
\begin{equation}
  \varsigma \;\equiv\; \frac{(1-\phi)(\bar R^S-\bar R^f)+\phi\,\overline{\mathrm{spread}}}{\bar R^f} ,
  \tag{C.34}\label{eq:D_varsigma}
\end{equation}
%
the coefficient recursions of Section~\ref{par:A322} collapse, exactly as before, to $\bar\nu^b=\bar\Omega^b$ and $\bar\eta^b=\varsigma\,\bar\nu^b$, with $\bar\Omega^b=(1-\sigma^b)+\sigma^b(\bar\nu^b+\lambda\bar\eta^b)$. Solving the two together,
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar\nu^b=\frac{1-\sigma^b}{1-\sigma^b\bigl(1+\lambda\varsigma\bigr)},
  \qquad
  \bar\eta^b=\varsigma\,\bar\nu^b,
  \qquad
  \theta^b=\bar\eta^b+\frac{\bar\nu^b}{\lambda}=\bar\nu^b\Bigl(\varsigma+\frac1\lambda\Bigr) ,
  \tag{C.35}\label{eq:D_nub}
\end{equation}
%
the last equality inverting $\lambda=\bar\nu^b/(\theta^b-\bar\eta^b)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:D_nub} is what makes the endogenous-$\lambda_t$ derivation of Section~\ref{par:A322} internally disciplined rather than free: given a target steady-state leverage $\lambda$ and the steady-state loan and bond margins, the implied diversion parameter $\theta^b$ is uniquely determined and can be compared with the values used in \textcite{Gertler2011}. The admissibility condition simplifies relative to a loan-only portfolio: $\sigma^b(1+\lambda\varsigma)<1$ is still required for $\bar\nu^b$ to be positive and finite, but $\bar\eta^b>0$ now holds for any $\phi\in[0,1]$ provided $\bar R^S>\bar R^f$ or $\overline{\mathrm{spread}}>0$ --- either wedge alone is enough, since $\varsigma$ is a weighted average of two individually positive terms rather than a single term scaled by $(1-\phi)$.

Home bias no longer dilutes franchise value at this calibration: since the promised-yield sovereign spread exceeds the bank loan spread ($\overline{\mathrm{spread}}\approx0.70\%$ against $\bar R^S-\bar R^f\approx0.20\%$ quarterly at the baseline calibration), $\varsigma$ is \emph{increasing} in $\phi$ --- a larger sovereign bond share raises, rather than dilutes, the bank's steady-state franchise value. A higher $\phi$ still requires a higher $\theta^b$ to hold leverage at the same target $\lambda$, but for the reverse reason to before: it is precisely because a more valuable franchise is more tempting to divert that a stronger deterrent is needed to keep the constraint binding at an unchanged leverage limit.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Public Authority}\label{sec:D9}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Using the real, discount-bond form of the government financing identity
employed in the consolidation of Section~\ref{sec:A5},
$T_t=R^{b}_tQ^{b}_{t-1}B^{b}_{t-1}-Q^{b}_tB^{b}_t$, detrended and
evaluated at the steady state with $\bar\Gamma^{-1}=e^{-\mu}$ and
$\bar b=\bar b^{b}$:
%
\begin{equation}
  \bar T
  =\bar Q^{b}\bar b\bigl(e^{-\mu}\bar R^{b}-1\bigr)
  =\bar b\,e^{-\mu}-\bar Q^{b}\bar b
  =\bar Q^{b}\bar b\left(\frac{e^{-\mu}}{\bar Q^{b}}-1\right) ,
  \tag{C.36}\label{eq:D_T}
\end{equation}
%
with the bond quantity recovered from the value already determined
in~\eqref{eq:D_bankBS}, $\bar b=\phi\lambda\bar n^{b}/\bar Q^{b}$.
Taxes are positive whenever $\bar Q^{b}<e^{-\mu}$, i.e.\ whenever the
real bond yield exceeds trend growth --- the usual $r>g$ condition for a
balanced-budget debt rollover to require primary revenue.  The Taylor
rule~\eqref{eq:A4_taylor} holds identically at $\bar r$
from~\eqref{eq:D_r} and imposes no further restriction.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Solution Algorithm and Summary}\label{sec:D10}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The steady state admits a fully recursive closed-form solution: no
numerical fixed point is required.  The ordering is

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Disaster moments and discounting.}
      $\Lambda^M$; $\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{K}$
      \eqref{eq:D_Dcal}--\eqref{eq:D_Kcal}; $\beta(\bar\theta)$
      \eqref{eq:D_betabar}; $\bar Q^{f}$ \eqref{eq:D_Qf};
      $\bar R^{f}=\bar R^{d}$ \eqref{eq:D_Rf}; $\Theta$
      \eqref{eq:D_Theta}.
\item \textbf{Sovereign block.} $\bar H^{b},\bar Q^{b},\bar R^{b},
      \mathbb{E}[\bar R^{b}],\overline{\mathrm{spread}}$
      \eqref{eq:D_Hb}--\eqref{eq:D_spread}.
\item \textbf{Nominal block.} $\bar\pi^{*},\bar\Omega,\overline{mc},\bar r$
      \eqref{eq:D_pistar}--\eqref{eq:D_r}.
\item \textbf{Rates and capital return.} $\bar R^{S}$ from the target
      bank margin; $\mathcal{F}$ from the target entrepreneur premium;
      $\bar P^{k,\mathrm{real}}$ \eqref{eq:D_pkr}; $\bar{\tilde R}^{K}$
      \eqref{eq:D_RK}; $\eta$ \eqref{eq:D_eta}.
\item \textbf{Real block.} $\bar{\tilde k}/\bar L,\bar w$
      \eqref{eq:D_kl}; per-labour ratios \eqref{eq:D_perL};
      $\bar L$ \eqref{eq:D_L}; then $\bar k,\bar y,\bar c,\bar i,
      \bar{\tilde k},\bar\xi_1,\bar\xi_2,\bar v$.
\item \textbf{Financial quantities.} $\bar k^{n}$ \eqref{eq:D_kn};
      $\bar n^{e}=\varkappa\bar k^{n}$; $f_0=\mathcal{F}\varkappa^{\chi^{e}}$;
      $\bar Q^{\mathrm{tob}}\bar s$ \eqref{eq:D_QS};
      $\bar n^{b}$ \eqref{eq:D_nb}; $\bar Q^{b}\bar b^{b},\bar d$
      \eqref{eq:D_bankBS}; $\iota^{e}$ \eqref{eq:D_iotae};
      $\iota$ \eqref{eq:D_iota}.
\item \textbf{Bank value function.} $\varsigma,\bar\nu^{b},\bar\eta^{b},
      \theta^{b}$ \eqref{eq:D_varsigma}--\eqref{eq:D_nub}.
\item \textbf{Fiscal residual.} $\bar b,\bar T$ \eqref{eq:D_T}.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Steps 1--3 are entirely independent of the banking block and
reproduce \textcite{Isore2017} whenever $\Delta^k$ and $\bar\theta$
enter; steps 4--7 are where the two financial wedges $\mathcal{F}$ and
$\bar R^{S}/\bar R^{d}$ displace their capital Euler.

\paragraph{Admissibility conditions.}
The calibration must satisfy
%
\begin{equation*}
\begin{array}{lll}
  \text{(i)} & \Theta=\beta(\bar\theta)e^{(1-\psi)\mu}<1
             & \text{finite household value function \eqref{eq:D_v}}\\[2pt]
  \text{(ii)} & \zeta\,\Theta\,(1+\bar\pi)^{\nu}<1
              \;\text{ and }\;\zeta(1+\bar\pi)^{\nu}<1
             & \text{convergent Calvo sums \eqref{eq:D_xi}, \eqref{eq:D_Omega}}\\[2pt]
  \text{(iii)} & \bar P^{k,\mathrm{real}}>\delta_0
             & \text{utilisation SOC, }\eta>1\text{ \eqref{eq:D_eta}}\\[2pt]
  \text{(iv)} & \sigma^{e}e^{-\mu}\bar R^{S}<1
             & \text{bounded entrepreneur net worth \eqref{eq:D_iotae}}\\[2pt]
  \text{(v)} & \sigma^{b}\bigl(1+\lambda\varsigma\bigr)<1
             & \text{bounded banker franchise value \eqref{eq:D_nub}}\\[2pt]
  \text{(vi)} & \bar R^{S}>\bar R^{f}\;\text{ and }\;\phi<1
             & \bar\eta^{b}>0,\text{ leverage constraint binds}\\[2pt]
  \text{(vii)} & \iota>0,\;\iota^{e}>0
             & \text{positive start-up transfers \eqref{eq:D_iota}, \eqref{eq:D_iotae}}
\end{array}
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Nesting.}
Two limits are worth recording explicitly.  At $\phi=0$ the sovereign bond disappears from the bank balance sheet: $\bar Q^{b}\bar b^{b}=0$, $\varsigma=(\bar R^{S}-\bar R^{f})/\bar R^{f}$, and~\eqref{eq:D_nbLOM} loses its bond term, so that the counterfactual steady state differs from the baseline only through the bank's portfolio composition and the implied $\theta^{b}$ --- not through any of the real aggregates, since $\bar R^{b}$ enters no other equation.  At $\bar\theta=0$ or $\Delta^k=0$ we have $\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{K}=1$, $\Lambda^M=1$, $\bar R^{f}=e^{\psi\mu}/\beta_0$, $\overline{\mathrm{spread}} =\bar R^{f}\,\bar\theta\Delta^b/(1-\bar\theta\Delta^b)$, since $\tilde\Lambda^M\to1$ together with $\mathcal{E}\to1$, and the whole disaster apparatus collapses; the residual banking block is then a standard \textcite{Gertler2011}--\textcite{Bernanke1999} steady state.

\section{Long-term bond structure}\label{sec:LongTermBond}

% ============================================================
% Long-duration bond derivation, complete
% ============================================================

The following long-term bond structure was derived against \textcite{Gabaix2012}, \textcite{Woodford2001}-style perpetuity bonds, and \textcite{Bocola2016}'s own use of long-term government debt.

\textcite{Bocola2016} confirmed to use long-term government bonds held by banks, thus the the mark-to-market channel is structurally native to his model. \textcite{Gabaix2012}'s resilience term $H_{i,t}$ is explicitly built to extend to multi-period assets, where the yield
curve is a headline result of his framework, not a side extension.
\textcite{Woodford2001} developed a geometrically-decaying-coupon perpetuity device used below. The bond return term including the payoff haircut, result in
%
\begin{equation*}
  R^b_{t+1} = \frac{(1-x_{t+1}\Delta^b)\bigl(1+\rho_b Q^b_{t+1}\bigr)}{Q^b_t}
\end{equation*}
%
where the pricing term is
\begin{equation*}
  Q^b_t = \mathbb{E}_t\bigl[Q_{t,t+1}(1-x_{t+1}\Delta^b)(1+\rho_b Q^b_{t+1})\bigr].
\end{equation*}
%
Solving for the steady state, the bond pricing term yields 
\begin{equation*}
  \bar Q^b = \frac{\bar H^b}{\bar R^f - \rho_b\bar H^b}
\end{equation*}
%
while the bond return at steady state results in
%
\begin{equation*}
  \bar R^b = \frac{(1-\bar\theta\Delta^b)\bar R^f}{\bar H^b}, \qquad \text{with} \qquad
  \overline{\text{spread}} = \bar R^f \cdot \frac{\bar\theta\Delta^b(\Lambda^M-1)}{\bar H^b}
\end{equation*}
%
Lastly, this alters the government budget constraint, which in real terms matches the bank's real bond pricing:
%
\begin{equation*}
  T_t + Q^b_t\bigl(B_{t+1}-\rho_b B_t\bigr) = (1-x_t\Delta^b)\,B_t
\end{equation*}

The hypothesis organising this paper is that \textit{realised sovereign default risk} depresses output and tightens credit conditions through two distinct, sequentially activated channels. The \textit{asset pricing channel} operates continuously: a rise in the perceived probability $\theta_t$ of sovereign default raises the sovereign risk premium demanded by bondholders and compresses bond prices \parencite{Gabaix2012, Isore2017}, independent of whether a default ultimately occurs. The \textit{financial accelerator channel} activates only upon a realised default: losses on sovereign holdings erode bank net worth, raising the external finance premium on loans to entrepreneurs and firms, tightening credit supply, and amplifying the output contraction through the standard \textcite{Bernanke1999}-style feedback loop, here activated through bank sovereign bond holdings. A realised sovereign haircut is therefore necessary, though the elevated perceived probability that precedes it is what the sovereign spread continuously prices.
